### Market Weston Fen

A tip off by a colleague drew my attention to Market Weston in Suffolk, a boggy 
reserve that has seen some apparent re-introductions for marsh fritillary. I had seen 
posts too of recent sightings on forums that had suggested indeed they were there, so it 
was worth a look. So on a stolen Friday, I made a quick dash over there, as a solo outing, 2
at about 11 am to see if I could find any.

The weather felt somewhat against me, with sunlight a rare commodity in the morning. 
I was chasing a sun gap leading out to the site, as I watched it trying hard to peek out 
from behind dark looming clouds. I navigated through the various small lanes leading up to 
the reserve, which was all very quaint, and actually the sun had started to come out more 2
when I arrived.

I parked up at the small off-road verge side, and donned my wellies for this one. Coffee 
in hand, I went in the first gate, over a short field and a gate, and suddenly I was in 
the midst of a well managed damp wildflower boggy meadow that had all manner of fantastic 
plants.

However, I was really after devil’s bit scabious, one of the main foodplants of marsh 
fritillary. I hadn’t seen any though and I was beginning to wonder if I was in the right 
place. I was not settled and a bit on edge that I was missing a something rather obvious 
elsewhere, perhaps of the other side of the reserve. The site itself is not very big and 
has one main path running though it. The meadow looked very good though, and when the sun 
came out, a bunch of species lept into action necataring on the banquet provided. 
Conversely, part of me also thought that this was right. Among the insect community, 
three burnet companion moths had showed very well, among other species such as small 
heath, common blue, painted lady, and quite a few four-spotted chaser dragonflies were 
on the wing, which I was greatly enjoying.

I decided to explore a bit more and then headed out away from the main meadow across 
the rest of the reserve to see what was there. On this jaunt, following the main path 
across the other side, the area became evemtually quite wooded. At that point I was more 
certain it was better back where I was. I had also made a detour onto a path that seemed 
seasonally active and was currently closed, so I had to turn back there also. A red admiral 
and holly blue were a highlight on this foray.

After a couple of treks around these other paths, including jogs to catch the sun patches 
(what must that have looked like in my wellies!), I came to the conclusion the boggy meadow 
area was definitely the best bet.

I spent a good hour then around the original meadow area, crouched on a boggy path, eating 
my cheese and pickle sandwiches, and finishing my coffee, I scanned and waited. I was using 
binoculars to search the inaccessible boggier sections further away from me. After a while, 
I wasn’t feeling it; it could be the marsh fritillaries are so low density here, or depend 
on local knowledge of specific paths that I was not aware of. I headed back towards the car 
and made my way back through the very first small meadow I had come through, noticing a 
cinnabar moth on the way, and another common blue. I had also spotted a further gate in 
the distance that was not on the reserve map. I walked a little bit in that direction, 
but had no desire to go potentially trespassing; it was a path that led deep into the 
rest of the reserve. I questioned whether in fact the main path that I had used perhaps 
wasn’t the only route. At this point I had to move on and I left without seeing any marsh 
fritillaries this day. However, I had a second site that I wanted to try for a totally 
different butterfly community, the chalk grasslands of Devil’s Dyke.

### Devil's Dyke

Devil’s Dyke in Cambridgeshire and Suffolk comes up repeatedly in butterfly circles. 
I had heard great things about this place, which is home to some very interesting classic 
chalk species. Devil’s Dyke is an ancient chalk escarpment, a defensive construction, 
likely Anglo Saxon, that runs from Reach to Woodditton. It is very close to where I live – 
half an hour from a collection of species that I may otherwise have to travel much further
afield to see. I knew that among the chalk species, there were adonis blue here, as people 
had logged them recently, with latge counts up to 20 or more back in May. I had also 
checked in advance about where best to access the site. It seemed for my purposes, 
the best entry point was by the Newmarket July racecourse entrance.

It is always a bit uncertain arriving at a new site, but this one was textbook and 
super easy to access. After parking down a small side road, I then walked out the to 
Dyke. Some small steps take you up onto the top, past a board that gives a bit more 
information about the site.

By now it was about 14:50. It was fairly warm, but the clouds had started to pull 
the covers over a bit although it was still quite bright. However, the wind was an 
issue. It was whipping over the top quite a bit. I was a bit concerned that would 
affect insect flight, but then I saw quite a few things that reassured me. A willow 
warbler was belting out a tune.

across. Then I had the idea to drop down to the side out of the wind further down, after 
the first embankment section ended. This actually takes you down onto the racecourse. And 
here on the slopes it was alive with common blues

community, but it was still buzzing. A burnet companion moths was also here – that was 
two from two for those at the sites today.

I had not seen adonis blue yet and so, like the marsh fritillary, it felt as though this 
was not going to be my day – a case of the blues. A small heath lifted my spirits.

I headed back up the leeward slope onto the top and decided to head back. There are several 
other sections of escarpment further down, but time was ticking on. A bit more relaxed, 
and deflated, I was getting back towards the start of Devil’s Dyke. I then decided to 
step down again back onto the slope on one of the parallel paths that runs along the 
slope, one last time, which I nearly didn’t do, eying the path ahead home. 
Then something caught my eye. A blue. A very bright blue. Just sat wings at 45 degree 
angle facing me and still out of the wind but on the windier side.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/MarketWeston_DevilsDyke/Adonis_blue_1.jpg"

Was this one? I snapped a load of pictures and still wasn’t fully sure. The ID is
specific on this one, with black indentations performing the white boarders of the wings, 
misaligned hindwing spots, and the really vivid blue, but otherwise ID from common blue
for males is sometimes tricky as there is overlap in some characteristics. But something 
about this one felt different.

As I moved around to try and get clearer shots, the vividness of the blue was so striking, 
and it was like nothing I had seen before, electric. Indeed this WAS Adonis blue as it
had all the hallmarks.

<figure class="report-photo-stack">

The chequered fringe was very apparent on the right forewing. The dots also offset more 
than common on hindwing underside and prominent spots on the underside of the forewing.

I could not believe it, having just been about to give up and go home! A fabulous male 
adonis blue. I had to run the ID by AI as well just to check but irecord gave it as Adonis. 
pushed it a bit too far with trying to get even closer in, as he then flew back across 
the slope. This was truly a mood lifter and unforgettable, species number 35. I was left 
thinking, how did I just see that, what are the chances. I wondered if somehow the state 
of relaxation or dropping the pressure of wanting to find it made a difference to how I 
looked at the habitat. Whatever the case, lucky or not, this was just the tonic I needed.

I shuffled back down the entrance path, as this really was quite close to the main entrance 
on the first main slope, which I also thought was quite remarkable. Ok this was not the 
giddy heights of 20 or so individual adonis blues some people had found in May at this 
section of Devil’s Dyke, but it was in keeping with a drop off in numbers in early to 
mid June. I headed off home, so satisfied, to review the pictures and upload the sighting 
to irecord.
