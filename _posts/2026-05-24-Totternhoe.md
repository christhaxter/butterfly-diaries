## Two skippers, a Duke and a Blue
### Totternhoe NNR and Quarry, Bedfordshire

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Small_blue.JPG"
       alt="Entrance sign to Toternhoe">
  <figcaption>Small blue along the entrance path</figcaption>
</figure>

It had been a hot spell of weather in mid May, and the first heatwave of 2026. 
This had come after a very cool and unsettled start to the month. Such a change in weather 
felt quite abrupt as we had ditched our unseasonable winter hats of the previous weeks for 
shorts and T-shirts. On the day we headed out to Totternhoe, it was predicted to be hot, up 
to probably 28 degrees, with light winds. A little on the warm side but otherwise good 
conditions for seeing butterflies. I had four or five target species in mind here: 
small blue, dingy skipper, grizzled skipper, Duke of Burgundy (!) and green hairstreak. 
It was quite an ambitious list to see all of those, but worth a try as we just about in 
the predicted flight windows.

We arrived at the site at around 09:20 after a stop at a motorway services nearby. I did 
not feel that well rested after a combination of excitement for the trip, and a bit of 
anxiety about the small car park being full and not being able to park. But these fears 
were not well founded, and we had no trouble finding a spot.

I had read great things about Totternhoe. As an old chalk quarry this site packs a lot of 
goodness into a small area, with a variety of different chalk micro-habitats that deserved 
exploration. We were rewarded on arrival as it was such a lovely place.

I had done my research too of the flora and fauna of the site – this was the first big 
trip out specifically to search for butterflies. My partner was with me on this occasion. 
I was excited to check out the two main areas of the reserve but we only had time for one 
of these as two hours was all we could manage. There are two parts to this site, one is 
more focused on the historic attraction at the fort and surrounding chalk grasslands, 
managed by the National Trust, and the other parts to the north and east are managed by 
the Wildlife Trusts including the quarry. We focused here on the quarry being a good place 
to look for some chalk-specialist rarities.

We headed in the main entrance from the car park up some steps that took us up a long a 
track beside a large field to our right-hand side.

Various dog walkers were about, including one asking if we had a dog, which was fun 
and a bit random. I guess at that time on a  Sunday morning, who else would be out there? 
But a birding treat was in store first, as a corn bunting was belting out a tune, among 
other species like lesser whitethroats and chiffchaffs. This was right alongside the path 
and in full view.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Corn_bunting.jpg"
       alt="Corn bunting">
  <figcaption>Corn bunting in full song</figcaption>

It only took about 15 minutes until we came across the first small blue sighting of many 
that day, this one being along this same track leading up to the reserve. This was also 
the best photo I ended up taking of them. They are so small and dainty, and this was also 
located a bit away from any obvious kidney vetch, their larvae food plant. That encounter 
feeling was the first of many I experienced this season. That moment of, ‘wait what’s that, 
is that one, omg it is!’ is something you want to replicate over and over and becomes quite 
addictive. This was also species number 31 on my lifetime list.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Small_blue.JPG"
       alt="Entrance sign to Toternhoe">

We reached the main reserve entrance after a right turn at the top of the field, where 
there was a board up welcoming us, always a good sign.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Totternhoe_intro_sign.jpg"
       alt="Small blue">
  <figcaption>Entrance sign to Toternhoe</figcaption>
</figure>

The opening area, once into the reserve, was quite sandy underfoot on the chalk, and 
contained parts with tightly cropped grass, among some scrubbier parts also. To our left, 
there were some small slopes here and there, inviting a scramble up to explore, if only 
there was infinite time available. Then a bit further down, we saw something dart up from 
the path in front of us, dingy skippers! Immediately species number 32, not that I was 
counting at the time. These were the first dingy skippers of a fair few we saw on the day, 
but these ones were dancing on the path in front of us, interspersed with periods resting 
out in the open. It was hard to get a good picture of them here given the light, but it was a 
great first encounter. Two of them spiralled up in front of us as well, and there may 
actually have been three of four.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Dingy_skipper_ground.JPG"
       alt="First of the dingy skippers">

Then it was a case of choosing which of the many paths to take. It was getting hot by now, 
and we needed to stop frequently for shade and water. Taking a left turn into the more 
inviting bushier paths, we scanned the edge habitats.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/One_of_many_paths.jpg"
       alt="One of many paths">

I had Duke of Burgundy on my mind as the main prize for Totternhoe with them doing quite 
well here. We came across another dog walker along this stretch, that we had actually met 
originally along the field path. He also mentioned the Dukes. He directed us a bit further 
around from where we were, so we heeded that advice and marched on. The locals had already 
seemed very friendly. We then saw a large blue dragonfly speeding by us, which was probably 
a blue emperor. Along the way we came across other nice common things such as brimstone, 
red admiral, and common blue. Familiar faces in unfamiliar places.

I continued searching for butterfly foodplants, cowslips, bird foot trefoil, kidney vetch 
and so forth. It was clear some plants had already gone over, and perhaps signs of things 
running early this season.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Vetch.jpg"
       alt="Horseshoe vetch">

By the side of one path was an inviting detour off the beaten track that I thought looked 
good, and boy was I right as just over a small hummock was a grizzled skipper nectaring on 
horseshoe vetch! I was stunned, and it was a very obliging photo subject, species 33.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Grizzled_skipper.JPG"
       alt="Grizzled skipper on horseshoe vetch">

</figure>

That was three of the four main target species I had wanted to see, already encountered, 
not that this was a box ticking session, as I enjoyed the habitat and just being in the 
realm of these specialist species. However, the last species we wanted to see needed both 
local knowledge of its habitat, and a bit of luck.

We made our way around the chalk paths winding here and there, and after several stops for 
water and fresh application of sun cream, we arrived at the main view point at the top that 
overlooked the quarry. This was steeper and deeper than I expected and the views from the 
top were quite spectacular.

The butterfly ‘E banks’ are up here too. These are constructions built in the shape of 
giant E letters of varying bearing orientation, giving microclimates and slopes facing 
different directions around the compass. They were built in 2021 in collaboration with 
the University of Cambridge, with butterfly conservation in mind to help the species here.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/E_banks.jpg"
       alt="The E-banks">
  <figcaption>Information board for the butterfly E banks</figcaption>
</figure>

It was quite hot there so we did not stop long, instead choosing a path that meandered 
along the side of the quarry heading down into shade into the scrubby thickets. 
The search was on though for our final target, the Duke of Burgundy.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Path_after_top.jpg"
       alt="Down to the quarry">
  <figcaption>Heading downwards around the quarry</figcaption>
</figure>

Across the other side we saw a chap on his own with a notebook. That is always a good 
sign, someone else is out here, a fellow enthusiast. As we continued down towards the 
bottom of the quarry, we gradually got closer to him. We came in and out of some slightly 
wooded area, and a speckled wood darted out to greet us, which was lovely, but not quite 
the Duke!

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Speckled_wood.JPG"
       alt="Speckled wood">
  <figcaption>Speckled wood</figcaption>
</figure>

Then we met the fellow enthusiast where we struck up a conversation. We swapped stories 
about our sightings of the day and he kindly shared his own sightings. He was an expert, 
a knowledgeable local. He indeed said it was an early year, and the Dukes had mostly gone. 
My heart had sunk a little when he mentioned this, but I really knew this really already 
from earlier signs, and from what I had heard about the season progression so far. 
He had also seen a green hairstreak, a species that continued to allude me so far this year. 
However, he shared that the best place for Dukes was at the bottom of the quarry, where 
an old motorbike was dumped years ago and still remains.

We eventually followed him in a socially-distanced kind of way, and I could see up ahead 
he was at the bottom near the motorbike, paused. We approached. He had found one! He 
pointed and there it was, a Duke of Burgundy, probably one of the last survivors, and a 
tattered one at that, with battle scars betraying a season well lived. Still, there he 
was perched on a small stem mid height and offered a very easy photograph.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Totternhoe/Duke_of_Burgundy.JPG"
       alt="Duke of Burgundy">

That was an incredible feeling, species 34, and four new species in a single day. 
We were very fortunate to have met him, and I question whether I would have come all 
the way down to the quarry bottom if we hadn’t have run into him. I also showed my 
grizzled skipper picture, as he had not seen one that day. Apparently we were also 
lucky to see that too, it also probably being a late one. Chuffed!

We then had to make our way back as the heat was becoming less and less tolerable. 
Nearing 11 am by this point, a slight nervousness crept in as I felt a bit lost as we 
made our way up from the quarry basin. However, we managed to find our way out and headed 
back through the paths onto some familiar territory. We decided to follow a more open side 
path back out towards the reserve entrance, which allowed us to speed up the pace a bit. 
I would’ve loved to spend all day here. Our new surveyor friend meanwhile dived back into 
the scrub on another path.

On the way back I managed to find a much more obliging dingy skipper, and took a better 
photo.

Other small blues grabbed our attention along the paths back, and we ended up seeing 
quite a few of those on the day, but taking no better photos than the first we came across. 
We took the path back along the first field, where we initially found the first small blue, 
and also past the same singing corn bunting who was outrageously close to us, and still full 
of song; what a fab bird.

Then that was us done. We headed back to the car with a feeling of total accomplishment, 
and a bit of relief to get out of the sun. We had seen four new species and this really 
set the tone for the rest of the 2026 season, igniting further desire to become acquainted 
with all the UK species that I had pondered as a small boy in various ID books.

We then headed off to the nearby Dunstable Downs for refreshments. I hadn’t been to 
Dunstable Downs since I was a kid, where I flew kites with my parents. This added an extra 
dimension of memory reflection and nostalgia to the day.
