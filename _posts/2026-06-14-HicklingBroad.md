## A tail to tell from Hickling Broad & Buxton Heath
### Hicking Broad, Norfolk

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/HicklingBroad_BuxtonHeath/Swallowtail_money_shot.jpg"
       alt="Swallowtail">
</figure>

Who doesn’t love a swallowtail, right? Within the UK, they are incredibly localised to sites deep 
in the Norfolk Broads, but I had seen so many pictures and pondered ID books as a child that I 
surprised myself that I hadn’t yet tried to see one in the UK. I had also volunteered at Wicken Fen 
where an attempted introduction was tried, so I was fairly familiar with them. I had also recently 
been down to southern France around Montpellier in September 2025 and found one there, being more 
widespread in Europe. But in the UK, I had not yet attempted a sighting. My partner and I decided 
to focus on Hickling Broad and Marshes, a renowned site for them. Managed by Norfolk Wildlife Trust, 
this site of reedbed and marshes harbours a wide array of wildlife, including milk parsley, their 
larval food plant.

A week spent pondering the weather forecasts however again had left question marks over our timing 
of the visit. Come the weekend, the wind was ‘moderate’, and visiting an exposed reed bed site 
with swaying reeds got me wondering if they would be on the wing or staying out of trouble. 
The original plan for Saturday I renegaded on, after seeing wind gusts over 30 mph predicted, 
and I felt a lighter wind would be more beneficial to see one of Britain’s best and rarest. 
This may have been over thought, but we gambled and delayed to Sunday anyway, as winds were 
predicted to be lighter, albeit still moderate.

Come Sunday, our old friend, the cloud cover returned to try and spoil the show. At least Norfolk, 
in particular nearer to the coast, looked good for clearer conditions in the morning than further 
inland. We drove out, and once we got past the darker skies looming over Norwich, the Broads 
direction was noticeably better, and as we arrived at Hickling, the cloud bank broke.

We had also timed our visit with a dedicted butterfly walk that was taking place at Hickling that 
day, among other showcase events. lt was therefore busy along the tiny roads, with overflow 
parking in effect, with staff guiding us to a space. The staff and volunteers were incredible 
and so helpful on the day. We parked up, walked through the visitor centre, and I became slightly 
distracted with a moth trap demonstration that was taking place, with the peppered moth taking 
centre stage.

With a comfort break done, we spoke to a staff member who told us all about the site. I asked 
about the swallowtails and they suggested the Spinney walk which cuts through some light woodland 
bordering the reedbeds and has a dense block of brambles along the path, notorious for bringing 
them in during late morning.

I missed taking a picture of the spot, but it was a grassy path in a light woodland edge next 
to the reedbed expanse, that was lined with thick bramble and honeysuckle.

It was about 10:40 by now. Well, it took about five minutes until we saw our first swallowtail. 
It was incredible! It danced majestically around the tops of the brambles through the wood, and 
back around us, briefly nectared and then landed right down on the path facing towards us. It 
was not the best photo angle but I started snapping away regardless. Apparently this isn’t too 
common for them to land on the deck like this, and posed almost purple emperor like.

Another walker from the other direction did the same with a large telephoto lens. With my modest 
and humbled 250 mm lens on my Nikon Z50 with its comical shutter sound (I had not put it to silent, 
which I really should’ve!) I continued to take a bunch of photographs before the swallowtail 
lifted and nectared again in several spots, this time giving improved views.

away. This one was the same first individual that we had seen and was still looping through the 
woodland and returning to the brambles. The group, like us, were stunned and as we had spent time 
there already let them pass through to see for themselves. It also landed on the ground again.

It was hard to leave them and move on around the rest of the reserve. I first headed back to get

I somehow then managed to misplace my other half, but after that we got moving again through the

The looping walk took us up to Bygraves Marsh, around the top by the boat departures and back 
around to the visitor centre.

It was actually quite hot now. The scope too was already becoming a bad idea. I didn’t use it at 
all and it instead became a workout for us both swapping it among our four shoulders. The walk out 
to Bygraves was great though, with a spring in our step and there was a good spot to search for a 
few waders on Brendan’s Marsh. Then something caught my eye as we were walking south toward hide C, 
and I looked back – it was a bittern in flight! It then landed out of sight, but, well that was 
spectacular too!

and showed well, alongside a family of cettis warblers. Marsh harriers, Cranes and so forth,
were also seen in the top section.

‘gold zone’.

Back at the visitor centre, we made some book purchases, and headed back to the car and had some 
lunch. This was a job well done and mission accomplished, species 38! Well part one anyway, as it 
was time to head to a second site on route home for another species.

### Buxton Heath

Straight after the swallowtails it was onto the next adventure. The site, Buxton Heath, managed 
by the Wildlife Trusts, was an acidic heathland where there had been sightings of silver-studded 
blue recently. On leaving Hickling we navigated our way slightly inland north of Norwich to the 
heath, and parked at a small gated entrance with space for a car or two. I forgot to photograph 
the site. But it was charming. Lazily, I had checked using AI, the best way to get out onto the 
heath to see the silver-studded blues. It advised us to head through an initial woodland section 
and ignore the main gate entrance. This side access apparently would bring us out onto the drier 
part of the heath where the blues were. We saw this exact trail and tried it. After about 200 m we 
were still very much in woodland bordering the heath and I was questioning my life choices being 
best friends with AI. However, the heath was appearing more to our right. I could see a small track 
through the trees that didn’t look particularly well-used but following it led directly out to heath.

Then, wow there were blues everywhere, spot on!

It was a fantastic double-header, and a quite well planned trip (not to brag) to see two rarer 
UK species. However, I had to gather my strength as the next day I had taken as leave to try an 
even more elusive target, the black hairstreak! For now we headed home riding high the crest of
