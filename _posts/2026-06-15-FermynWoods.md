## Betting on black at Fermyn Woods Country Park
### Fermyn Woods, Northamptonshire

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/FermynWoods/2026_06_15/Black_hairstreak_5.JPG"
       alt="Black hairstreak">
</figure>

One species that had tickled my fancy this year was the black hairstreak, a blackthorn thicket, 
canopy dweller, notoriously hard to track and find, let alone photograph. Fermyn Woods had 
cropped up as a site good to try, not too far from us, and about 35 to 40 mins from my mums house 
a few towns over. The blackthorns there seemed to be fantastic, dense and on hilly ground allowing 
potential eye-height shots, although some folks had reported sightings nectaring on bramble recently 
as well.

This was a solo outing and I set off from home early that day. I also made a pit stop at my mums 
to break up the journey, not that it was a long expedition, being only an hour for me. However, 
the traffic had conspired against me; it was rush hour, Monday morning, so as I took annual leave, 
others battled to get to work, and the back fen roads from Ely were far worse than usual with a 
road closure, and a jacknifed lorry to contend with. I had lost so much time already. It was 
nearer 09:30 to 10:00 by the time I reached Huntingdon. But after a quick refreshment, I headed 
out to Fermyn still in good spirits.

The rest of the journey was a breeze. But the weather again was not great – a recurring theme of 
these posts. Cloud had rolled in after the previous day at Hickling, and it was predicted to break 
a little to allow some sunlight a bit later, particularly in the Corby area where I was headed. 
But on the way out, raindrops! Not a good sign, and the temperatures were only about 16 degrees, 
and so were not yet ideal, needing to climb a bit more to allow good butterfly conditions. However 
as I drove, again with one eye on the clouds, safely safely, the skies were thinning as I turned 
off the A14 and reached the spatious car park at Fermyn Woods Country Park. The site is amazing, 
with a cafe, toilet, childrens play area. About 11 ish by now I paid my car parking and headed out 
to the blackthorns via a quick check of the recent sightings board, noting with interest the black 
hairstreak sightings!

site and the species there, this one being for butterflies.

I reached the thicket in good time and having read the great instructions for the site online 
about where to go for particular butterfly species, I was quietly confident, albeit still a bit 
tired from yesterday’s antics with swallowtails and silver-studded blues at Hickling and Buxton. 
However, little did I know, this was about to be a long haul and a totally different sort of 
experience.

I meandered back and forth through the thicket along many paths, scouting out good sections, 
open glades, places where hairstreaks could bask. Nothing. I was getting a bit confused too as 
I kept coming across hawthorn instead of blackthorn and rather dense shaded sections that were 
clearly not what I was after. Then more drizzle arrived. Even though I could see blue sky above. 
I starred out from a vantage point and admired the wonderful views as I contemplated my next move.

I kept on, and doubled back and forth over the same spots quite a few times, and came up dry. 
This was beginning to feel improbable. I saw a volunteer busy doing some work in one area off the 
path, but I moved swiftly on by unseen so as not to disturb them. I consulted the maps again 
scratching my head, and stood beside the black hairstreak noticeboard in the thicket, but it was 
fairly quiet for insects at that particular spot. Marsh tits and bullfinches had been calling quite 
a bit, which was nice. But it was clearly either a bit early in the day and/or not quite warm 
enough yet up here.

As a bit more time passed, the weather was slowly improving, and a few butterflies were out in 
some sheltered spots. The spits of rain had stopped thankfully. I decided to sit down and have 
some lunch and coffee to regroup.

After lunch, and further doubling back and forth over my tracks again, I ventured out of the dense 
thicket, and headed to the edges of the scrub a bit more. I had been there already but one spot in 
particular I liked and had been over a couple of times. I changed tactics a bit and descended 
down one of the paths out of the blackthorn area up on the hill to the main tracks. My thinking 
was to try and target an area of brambles next to the long pond area. This was because I recalled 
some people had seen black hairstreaks many days earlier, as also confirmed on the noticeboard. 
I spent a good long while here, searching flowers, sitting on benches to rest legs, and supping 
my coffee.

A ringlet raised my spirits. But still no hairstreak had shown itself. I was also the only 
one around, so perhaps this was all a bit unlikely.

I found myself right by the entrance, or nearby at least, to Fermyn Woods itself. The thicket 
borders the mixed oak woodland and I even headed out of a gap in the trees as well that took me 
just outside the woodland complex, where I was suddenly next to loads of brambles and other flowers 
bordering large arable fields. This looked good for butterflies in general. There were quite a 
few large skippers flying here, but of course no hairstreaks. In hindsight that was clutching at 
straws, and it was never going to be the place where they were, but it was a nice area to have 
frequented.

I was just about defeated at this point. I figured I may as well go and have a look in Fermyn 
Woods itself taking the main path in – I had one eye on a later trip for purple emperors here, 
plus, I half wondered if it was too early for white admiral or silver-washed fritillary. Answer, 
yes it was. I walked a bit into the wood to check out spots on the map that were recommended for 
emperors, but the walk to get to the first crossroads mentioned in the online documentation was 
longer than I expected, probably a good 500 m. At the crossroads, I looked left in the direction 
of where it was recommended to turn to look for pueple emperors; note taken and mental marker made. 
That path was quite churned up, presumably by horses being a bridleway.

I had been on my feet for probably over four hours by this point. So I retreated heading back on 
the same path back to the edge of the wood and the blackthorn thicket area again.

I was now feeling almost fully defeated. I found myself back by the thicket again. The sun was 
out a bit more now, 15:00, and warmer too. I was back at one of the entrances to the blackthorns, 
and at that point I looked uphill to where I had been late morning onwards, and also straight 
ahead. It was an easy walk back to the car and then home. Choices, choices. One last try? Why not.

I ascended again higher up the path leading uphill into the blackthorns, and then I was suddenly 
nearby one of the glade areas I had earmarked originally as promising, with loads of mature 
blackthorn nearby, with some younger blackthorn too.

Various other insects I recall were there too. Then, just a bit higher up on a blackthorn 
leaf something somehow caught me eye, to this day I cannot explain how. Just sat on a leaf. 
A brown triangle. After a couple of seconds, I realised, is that one? It was partially obscured 
and I was viewing it from an angle below. Could it be? (Re-living this always sends a tingle 
down my spine).

<figure class="report-figure">

It really was! After raising my camera I took a dozen or so shots and the zoom in easily 
confirmed it was a black hairstreak! It is a feeling that is hard to explain. I had spent 
well over four hours on my feet covering the same areas so much so that if you had been GPS 
tracking my movements it would drawn a dense black blob on a map. But this really was one. 
I snapped a few more photos but it was at an awkward view, and I could not see the whole 
butterfly. It was missing its hind wing ‘tail’ and a bit tattered but I rotated around a bit 
further and got some further diagnostic ID shots.

I pushed it too far though as I spooked it so it took flight. I followed it back along the 
thicket path but lost it as it flittered back into to the deeper blackthorn scrub.

Stunned. I was on cloud nine. I was about to quit and go home and suddenly I had achieved the 
goal! It was the adonis blue from Devil’s Dyke all over again but dialled up to 11. My senses 
having been at max setting for hours were now dropped. I relaxed fully.

Along the path I was on, where I had skipped along chasing the hairstreak, I came across the 
volunteer I had seen earlier. I was now in a very chatty mood and brimming with elation and he 
straight off the bat asked me if I had seen any hairstreaks. This was minutes after my encounter 
and so I told him what had just happened, “I just saw the first one now, after nearly five hours!”. 
He alluded to the fact that it was possibly the last one, as the flight season had been so early 
this year, reminiscent of the surveyor we had met at Totternhoe. I had known this really, and it 
also tallied with the high number of sightings of black hairstreaks seen at the end of May 
displayed on the entrance noticeboard. I was aware of their relatively short flight window too. 
But perhaps I hadn’t fully appreciated just how at the edge of the distribution I really was, 
and how quick the tail off for some species is. So this really was needle in a haystack stuff, 
which began to slot into place – not that in peak time they are easy, as you could very easily 
still miss them, but somehow this made sense with the effort I put in, and also made the sighting 
feel even more remarkable. We talked about lots of things and I had asked him about what he did 
there, and as it turns out he was removing invasive plant species of various sorts to stem 
incursions of unwanted things; a very knowledgeable chap. A large red damselfly had perched by us, 
as well as a fly species I forgot the name of, but both were talking points.

He also said how the march of the brown hairstreak could bring them also to Fermyn! Brown 
hairstreaks also like blackthorn, as well as ash, so that was mentally logged as well. We parted 
ways, and I now really did head for the car park, still stunned and a bit bemused. I swung by the 
meadow area as well, that has a nice lizard monument, and I had also seen a lizard at some point 
on my travels on one of the paths basking in the sun – not the only below though.

On route back I swung by the original paths that lead up to the blackthorns, past another 
noticeboard, again noting a prominence of black hairstreak documented. I didn’t see turtle dove 
or hawfinch this time though! I noted grizzled and dingy skippers were here too, maybe that was 
one for the future to complement our recent experience at Tottenhoe, and also handy for green 
hairstreak, which at the time of writing I had yet to photograph.

lady. There were already beginning to be a lot of painted ladies around this year.

I returned with tales of my adventures, as if this was something out of a Lord Of The Rings novel. 
It is is hard to convey these things and perhaps only naturalists may fully appreciate that balance 
of prep time, fieldcraft, weather watching, tracking tactics, sheer grit and determination with 
a dash of good fortune, that leads to these moments of sheer elation. It is the reason I think I 
am so invested in this hobby as it delivers such an adrenaline rush, a dopamine hit that is rather 
addictive. I had to remember to prepare for the times when no doubt you may come up dry, and 
hairstreaks can be extremely tricky species to meet. This also seemed like a run of good fortune 
though hot off the back of the adonis blue experince at Devil’s Dyke that had a similar last 
minute success vibe. I confess looking back, I hadn’t played this one perfectly, as the weather 
was not sufficient early on, so I should’ve probably held back a couple of hours, but then again 
that did give valuable reccy time and I remembered the best spots for later in the day. Overall, 
I enjoyed the thrill of the chase and seeing species number 37, although easier said in hindsight. 
I vowed to return again in another year to try again for black hairstreak when I am more in the 
peak of the distribution, to enjoy the species more, and to improve on the photos. But I had to 
admit that this was job done for now and I shifted sights to the next target.
