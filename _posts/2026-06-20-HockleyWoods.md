## Heath-seeking at Hockley Wood
### Hockley Wood, Essex

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Hockley_Woods/Heath_fritillary_pair.JPG"
       alt="Heath Fritillary pair">
</figure>

The idea to go to Hockley was always an early decision and locked in the calendar, knowing the 
site has a thriving population of heath fritillary. Located in Essex, this was in reach of us for 
a quick day tripper with my partner also joining me for this adventure.

I had weather anxiety issues again for this weekend, with so much more cloud pushing in compared 
to the week, although the temperatures were ok. And for once we wanted to actually go on a Saturday 
rather than a Sunday. The travel down south was ok, in fact better than I expected, and the wood 
was straightforward to find after navigating through the villages.

The clouds broke a little more after we arrived at the site around 09:30, quite early. A park run 
was taking place when we got there. It was great to be among the early morning revellers. I had 
recalled seeing a park run symbol on the woodland map too.

We headed off south and east in the woodland. It was a fabulous mature mixed woodland, called 
Great Bull Wood – I had read that the best spots for heath fritillaries were in the southern part 
of the wood (I think named Hawkwell Wood, and Parson’s Snipe) in the wide open glades that are 
coppiced and managed specifically for this species. The wood is managed by Rochford District 
Council with conservation work in collaboration with Natural England and the Forestry Commission.

The woods were quite inviting. For heath fritillary, we had to look for common cow-wheat, the 
caterpillar food plant and the open glades. We therefore set off on a path that led by the 
playground on the left.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Hockley_Woods/Car_park_view.jpg"

The path meandered within the eastern flank of the wood. For a while this was deep and darker 
woodland but a little further on the canopy opened up a bit more and some nice glades looked 
promising.

I then saw a flash of orange high up. This wasn’t a comma, that typically pops up when looking 
for other orange things, sorry commas. This was a silver-washed fritillary! It dashed very fast 
high up. And then it disappeared. We stayed for a few minutes but couldn’t relocate it, and so 
moved on though. Along this path was a really nice glade with cow-wheat, but nothing in the shape 
of a heath fritilary initially leapt out.

We pushed on ahead. Two guys were approaching from the other direction, engrossed in deep chat. 
They were carrying cameras and then as they passed by I politely interjected to ask them about 
the heath fritillaries. They pointed us around the southern wood path where we were headed, which 
was very reassuring news.

Further down the route, at the southern juncture, we were turning to the west along the southern 
path when a flash of a larger dark butterfly flying high caught my eye – it was a white admiral! 
It was fast and sometimes decending down to the understory, but was not really settling. After a 
few minutes doubling back up and down the corner section of the path, we lost sight of it. It was 
amazing to have already seen two classic woodland species already in the space of just a few 
minutes. However, I had not had the chance to grab a photo of either. That played on my mind a 
little. Not to worry, the sightings themselves were amazing.

We moved further along the southern section. This section I think was the Parson’s Snipe part of 
the wood, and here we passed increasing amounts of bracken. Speckled woods, large skippers, and 
other species such as ringlets and meadow browns were making an appearance.

We paused and scanned. And suddenly I caught sight of it. Just sat low down basking on a leaf, there 
it was! Heath Fritillary!
  
<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Hockley_Woods/First_heath.JPG"

It was so beautiful and one of the best ‘omg’ moments we’ve had. It was a peaceful moment as well, 
just us, the quiet wood, and these amazing butterflies. I took loads of great pictures of this one, 
as it was so obliging. I could get very close to them. Then I spotted another also basking nearby, 
and then another further in nearer the trees. This was amazing. It was as if the more you looked, 
the more you saw.

They were enjoying basking in the warming temperatures and slight rays of sun now peeking through. 
By arriving quite early, we had probably timed it so they were in a warm up phase. I also managed 
a rather cool double shot as a heath fritillary and a speckled wood shared a single flower head.

We had done it though, finding species number 40, by researching the site, reading the habitat 
structure and food plant. This felt like a deeper satisfaction of connecting ecologically with 
the species and the conservation management that allows them to flourish. It was also a complete 
contrast with the five-hour endurance exercise I had experienced for black hairstreak in the days 
before. Common cow-wheat is also quite a pretty plant as well.

<figure class="report-figure">

The ones we saw here in the glade then also took flight in a gentle flutter. I really enjoyed 
this species as it has so much charisma, and is photo friendly. We walked a bit further along 
but didn’t see a better spot any further down than the first one we found…yet.

We figured this was mission accomplished and so my attention turned to seeing if we could track 
down the white admiral and silver-washed fritillary that we encountered earlier. We back-tracked 
the same way we came to the ‘white admiral’ junction, where the path turned north, but couldn’t 
see it this time.

Moving further back the way we came, one of the glades I spotted more heath fritillaries! This 
was interesting as we had passed this spot originally. Perhaps they had been tucked away similar 
to the other glade. Here again there was cow-wheat and the sun was hitting more now, albeit clouds 
were still coming and going. There were loads of the fritillaries in this one spot.

It was amazing and I had so many great photos snapped. I counted up to 15 individuals on the day, 
and that was probably an underestimate.

A very small distance from here, essentially part of the same glade really, was the place we 
originally saw silver-washed fritillary and there it was again! This time it seemed to be landing 
and nectaring a bit more than previously. At first it wouldn’t settle. I was pacing up and down 
the path tracking it until finally it stopped at some distance away on bramble. And here, I took 
one of my favourite pictures with the fritillary set perfect against a dark backdrop of the wood 
with a decent aperture. I confess that was taken on an auto setting though.

Then a fantastic bit of luck, a white admiral landed! I was not sure if it was the same one that 
we had previously. It the landed a bit further along the ride, perched on a bramble leaf, still at 
some distance. I managed to grab a decent photograph, cropped, and not the sharpest image ever 
taken. However, I was thrilled with it in the circumstances. Ten seconds later it took off.

I think there were at least a couple of silver-washed fritillaries around as well. This goes to 
show pausing, observing the areas and waiting for things to reveal themselves was always a good 
tactic.

After that, we pressed on back north towards the car park. We ending up going a slightly different 
way back down the main spine of the woodland on a wider track. Various other commas and whites too 
were around.

I try to document even common species where I can with a picture, good also for irecord. For the 
main woodland specialists, we were now done and we were on the home stretch.

We came across a lady with a cool camera. It turned out that she was just like us, here for the 
heath fritillaries and was actually a little lost. I pointed her exactly where we had come from, 
explained a bit about the species answering her question, and my other half joked that I now 
sounded like a local expert, “oh yeah yeah just go right down there, turn right, look for common 
cow-wheat, you can’t miss em”. Well I said it slightly differently, but you get the idea. It is 
also quite interesting how we all pass the information on in a sort of relay fashion, paying it 
forward and helping each other out. It felt good to be part of that.

Back at the car, we had lunch, and acquired some Fanta cans from a handy stall we had parked next 
to gave us some needed sugar. A couple of horse riders and other woodland goers passed by. We 
were originally thinking of a quick scout around the northern and western part of the wood, but 
we were done. After a quick scan of a buddleia in the main car park vicinity, we decided to leave 
it at that. The clouds had rolled over more now too. It had already been a highly successful day 
so we chose to end on a high.

We swung by a garden centre on the route back to the M25. Aptly we picked up a set of butterfly 
wall metal ornaments that I put up, decorating a seating area with this year’s theme!
