## Seeing green on return to Devil’s Dyke
### Devil's Dyke, Suffolk

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_06_21/Dark_green_fritillary_best.JPG"
       alt="Dark green fritillary">

This chalk escarpment had much more to offer with further species coming through at different 
stages of the season. After the early species had done their thing, including the first brood 
of Adonis blue, it was time to look for dark green fritillary. With the season running quite 
ahead this year, I felt my timing was about right for this one.

These orange missiles are known to fly over the open grassland as males defend patches and search 
for females. It would be amazing to come across them.

I knew the site well now, the parking, the entrance, the steps up to the top. It was about 10:00 
when I got to the site on this occasion. I  marched on, up to the top of the Dyke, and encountered 
the same first meadow slopes dotted with patches of scrub that I was now quite familiar with. 
This was a nicer day too than last time, with sunny spells and less wind.

The initial slopes were buzzing with life, with a nice little moth that I hadn’t seen before, 
a common heath, that I had to descend a little lower to get nearer for a picture.

Back up on the top main chalk path, and looking back down the slope, marbled whites were aplenty, 
in fact they were everywhere, matings too – this was obviously a peak time for them.

I descended back down a bit further to check out flower sources where dark greens may be nectaring. 
No luck yet. It was time for the first of a number of ‘oh is that one?’ ‘Oh no it’s a comma’, 
moments. They are lovely butterflies but as with some of my other visits, they have a habitat of 
turning up when you looking for something else orange. One in fact whistled by quickly whilst I 
was checking the thistles, and to be honest, it could’ve been a fritillary, but the fact I saw a 
comma just moments later made be err on the side of caution.

I had a brief rest on the slopes and then headed back up past some shrubs to the top.

As covered in my last post about Devil’s Dyke, the path dips down after sections of the embankment, 
with an option to go back onto the Newmarket racecourse, and then the path ascends back onto onto 
the top again for the next embankment section. The first section of the Newmarket end starts and 
ends quite scrubby with trees nearby, albeit with nice meadow slopes too in between. The next 
section becomes more open as you walk further on, where the meadow keeps widening on the slopes, 
and eventually loses some of the scrub, although it has some coniferous sapplings dotted in 
amongst the grasses as well. But then I spotted another orange butterfly. Could it be?
  
It was!
  
I had a really good view of this one as it came quite close. It was very flighty. It was 10:30 
by this point. I whipped out the camera, but it wouldn’t settle. I had time to admire the 
intricate markings, but it then flew over onto the racecourse, back across the Dyke, and then 
on further. No photo, but wow! However, just a bit further down, I saw another, and then 
another! They were dashing all over this slope to the west side.

I kept waiting for them to stop flying or pause at least, but they never did. I started then 
trying to photograph them in flight, increasing my shutter speed. This visit essentially turned 
into a tracking and in flight photography session. I must’ve taken hundreds of pictures. But, a 
few made the cut.

At the end of the second section of the escarpment, I crossed over the other side of the dyke 
so I was now on the west side on the side of the racecourse. The problem I had, aside fom them 
not stopping, was the steepness of the slope and tracking them as I could not move closer too 
them on the incline efficiently. Across the other side was flatter, and a few were still 
patrolling over there among various flowers in the meadow. I got a tiny bit closer to a couple.

It was about midday by this point, so that was a good two hours of concentration, tracking them, 
snapping away, but I was getting tired. It felt right to leave it there. I have to say, to not 
get ‘the’ shot of a perched one with a crisper image was a slight pang. But I was really pleased 
to have shared the slopes with them, species 41, and they certainly lived up to their behavioural 
speedy quirks.

I made my way back along the side of the racecourse mostly. Actually I did divert again back onto 
the Dyke through an opening, to then bring me back to the very first slope, the adonis blue slope 
as I feel I should call it. But the sequel to Devil Dyke, ‘the return’, had been very successful, 
14 butterfly species were found overall, including the orange bullet. That was also three 
fritillary species I had seen in two days, with the silver-washed and heath fritillaries at 
Hockley yesterday. I vowed to try and return to Devil’s Dyke at some point later in the season, 
maybe in a couple of weeks, to see if I could get a better picture of a dark green – one 
nectaring would be nice.
