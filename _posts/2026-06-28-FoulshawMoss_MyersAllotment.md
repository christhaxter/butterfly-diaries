## Foulshaw Moss, Friday 26 June

A last minute switch of our plans left heading north to the Lake District to see family. 
Originally we were going to target large blue at Rodborough Common, but plans had to shift. 
However, this offered an opportunity to piggy-back a couple of butterfly trips to see some things 
that were not originally part of the 2026 plan, and a chance for large heath and northern 
brown argus.

We had annual leave on the Friday, and I decided that, given we were staying at Lancaster, a 
short hop north to a site, Foulshaw Moss, was in reach to try for large heath. But to get there, 
and spend time there, an early start was needed. The Friday was the last day of a second UK 
heatwave of 2026 and it was predicted to break.  This week had been exceptional, with oppressive 
35 degree heat hitting the UK. The weather was going to be hottest in the country in East Anglia 
on the Friday, so leaving to head northwest felt like a good decision. To give enough time there, 
we set off before 8 am, aiming to be there at 1 pm.

The journey was stifling. Worried also about the car getting us there, we pootled our way across 
the country, and took several stops to break the journey. The temperatures hit 35 degrees along 
the way. The journey was therefore long, and delayed, but the Lakes were a welcome view, and for 
a flat fenlander like myself, are always jaw dropping.

We arrived at Foulshaw Moss, after navigating a broken down coach causing tailbacks on the A591. 
It was still 30 degrees at the site when we arrived, very exceptional heat for the north.

Foulshaw Moss is a boggy site managed by Cumbria Wildlife Trust that have spent years managing 
tree plantations and blocking drainage ditches to restore it as a lowland peat bog. This was 
well worth a look as it holds good numbers of a species I had not seen before, and the large heath 
is associated exactly with these habitats.

On arrival, we figured we could really only spend around 30 mins there in those conditions. The 
entrance road to the site was a narrow but easy access road off the main road that we had passed 
many times when we had been in the Lakes in the past.

Sweating, we made our way to a CWT employee heroically manning a small entrance stall. He was 
doing amazing given the heat and gave us the introduction speech. Osprey was the main focus, 
fabulous birds, and two cameras were trained on nests. We inquired also about the large heath 
too. I was also very keen to see white-faced darter dragonflies, a rare and localised species 
specific to bogs.

We originally headed out along a boardwalk path to the right, leading to a pond area, along 
which there were large skippers. That was great, but with every minute precious, I really 
wanted to get out onto the Moss proper where the large heaths were likely present. This actually 
sadly meant sacrificing the dragonflies, and I wondered they may have been in that pond area.

We made our way back by the main entrance, and this time went up the main path. A couple of 
minutes later we were out onto the boardwalks on the main heath.

I got a first impression that the bog was quite dry, given the temperatures, although that 
was obviously a bit of red herring given how bogs work underneath.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Foulshaw_Moss/Heather.jpg"

But then I almost imediately spotted a few butterflies over the Moss. I wasn’t 100% sure at first 
but following a few it was clear they were large heaths!
  
They were flying quite near the edge of the bog only, near to where the scrub and ferns met the 
open heather and sphpagnum. Perhaps it was a bit cooler (i.e. not as stifling) there that out 
in the open expanse with opportunities for shelter.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Foulshaw_Moss/Ferns.jpg"
       alt="Ferns and scrub meeting heather at the edge of the Moss">
  <figcaption>Ferns and scrub meeting heather at the edge of the Moss</figcaption>
</figure>

However, they, did, not, stop, flying. This felt like dark green fritillaries all over again 
and I was in no fit state to start experimenting with my camera shutter speeds in a heatwave. 
I was both elated to find them, but disappointed a little that I may come away without a picture 
souvenir. I therefore snapped a total of seven bad pictures, and came away feeling like that was 
pointless even raising my camera. Most of the images were therefore a blur or just bog. At least 
we had come across them.

However, in the car later, after leaving the site, I let out a small yelp when going through the 
images. One of the seven, at the top, actually showed a large heath. Badly out of focus, but very 
clearly diagnostic – a great ID of the underwing!
  
<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Foulshaw_Moss/Large_heath.JPG"
       alt="A large heath in mid flight">

It was ridiculously fortunate, as I had indeed got a picture, of sorts, that even irecord was 
able to assign the ID – you can see the dark black circles on the hindwing just about – frozen 
(bad choice of word) mid flight over the Moss in a heatwave, and species number 42.

The temperatures at this point were now quite unbearable in the sun. Another chap passed us on 
the boardwalk, coming from out in the middle of the heath, which I wondered to myself how he 
wasn’t melting. We had actually walked further onto the boardwalk but had turned back. We 
instead headed back down the track that led to car park, covering probably only 200 
metres and feeling like it was 200 miles in this heat.

We did, however, have just enough energy in the tank to visit the ospreys. The Wildlife Trusts 
chap had showed us the route to one of the fixed cameras, not a long walk, so we headed there 
turning right at the bottom of the car park and after passing some other people – yes we were 
not the only people braving these temperatures – we reached two other Wildlife Trust folks who 
were manning a scope trained on an osprey nest.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Foulshaw_Moss/Osprey.JPG"
       alt="Distant view of one of the osprey nests">
  <figcaption>Distant view of one of the osprey nests</figcaption>

That was really amazing. There were two nests at Foulshaw, one with three chicks one with two. 
You could see them too through the fixed scope.

We then headed back to the car after a very brief chat with the folks manning the osprey scope. 
It was a shame not to fully appreciate this amazing site. We hadn’t seen the dragonfly, the large 
heaths were there but we hadn’t photographed them cleanly, and so it gave us fuel to hoepfully 
return. That said, overall in the short time we were there and under the conditions we were faced 
with, this was a big achievement.

After leaving the site, the heatwave broke. Literally as soon as we departed and we were back in 
the M6 heading back south to Lancaster, almost to the hour, the temperatures took a near 10 degree 
drop as more cloud also rolled in. Thoughts turned to “if we had just set off later from home”, 
and “could we have spent longer there and got better pictures” etc were inevitably spinning in 
my head, and needed quietening.

Back at the hotel, I was tired and had a nap before switching from ecologist mode to uncle mode 
that evening and the following day. I was hoping, but not certain, that we still may have time 
for another butterfly visit somewhere.

## Myers Allotment Sunday 28 June

It had been a busy but enjoyable Saturday with the family. It had involved taking the scenic route 
from Lancaster to the southern Lakes, a Lake Windermere car ferry, a visit to Hawkshead, lunch, 
a swing back by Windermere to visit relatives and a return back to Lancaster via Morecambe Bay.

Come Sunday after saying our goodbyes at around 10 am, we were left with a couple of hours to do 
something with before heading back down south.

I had already spied a few other places we could go. A couple of sites caught my attention nearby 
in the general Carnforth, Arnside and Silverdale area. For example, Warton Crag, a a limestone 
hill near Carnforth and looked amazing, maybe for northern brown argus, high brown fritillary 
maybe pearl bordered fritillary. Also, Gait Barrows looked great as well for woodland species, 
maybe also high brown fritillary. We could not do everything in two hours after energy reserves 
were already depleted somewhat from the previous two days. Warton Crag, perhaps too much uphill? 
Another time maybe. Another site, however, Myers Allotment looked interesting and funny enough, 
we had past by there on route north from Lancaster the day before, being next door to the famous 
Leighton Moss RSPB reserve.

Myers Allotment is a site owned by the Leighton Hall estate leased by Butterfly Conservation 
managed specifically with butterfly conservation in mind. Here, we had a real chance to see 
northern brown argus. This species likes common rock rose, a low growing species with yellow 
flowers, the caterpillar food plant, which grows well on limestone areas, and Myers has these 
habitats. There are also great wooded spaces here, not a big site by any means at seven hectares, 
so high brown fritillary was possible but would require careful separation from dark green 
fritillaries in the field. But it was quite early in the season for them. Frustratingly so, 
as possibly by only a week or two. Small pearl bordered? Maybe, but actually likely too late here. 
So we were actually visiting at a difficult middle spot between two species’ flight windows. 
Nevertheless, we packed up and headed to Myers.

The parking for this site is best at Leighton Moss, and then a short walk to get to Myers. Sadly, 
there was no time to visit Leighton, but once boots were on, we inquired with staff how to get to 
Myers, a three minute walk.

We saw an entrance to Myers Allotment. It looked like a luscious wilderness buzzing with life. 
The entrance we were at was quite bushy and my first thought at this point was, ok can I really 
do this given I had shorts on and was tick-aware. But, I braved it. The paths although well 
defined were also fairly luscious, although in the distance I could hear a strimmer going so 
there was someone out here doing some maintenence work.

I was in search mode. We came across a small limestone exposed grassy area that I had a quick 
look at. After the fact I think brown argus were probably here. A dragonfly buzzed by that I
missed. We were generally in quite deep woodland from the start, lots of bramble, ferns and so 
forth. Lots of browns were dancing around, ringlets, meadow browns, skippers too.

At this point I hadn’t fully accepted the fritillaries were going to be a no show so I was 
on orange butterfly alert. Commas however were the only ones willing to show themselves and 
at least three painted ladies were on the wing.

<figure class="report-figure">

We continued our way on the looping route around the site which took us up a hill, with a slight 
feeling of not wanting to get lost or turned around, knowing we had a time limit.

Eventually we came out on a higher spot as the path began to descend back down and the area 
opened up. Indeed this was a good looking spot. But, the horseflies on this day were, I have 
to say, a nightmare. We had to resort to some generous application of jungle formula that we 
probably should’ve realised we needed beforehand. I then spotted a small brown argus like 
butterfly. Could this be?

Yes!
  
<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Myers_Allotment/Northern_brown_argus1.JPG"
       alt="First view of northern brown argus">

I could see the white spots on the upper forwings were prominent although still a bit uncertain. 
This site is regarded, however, as only having northern brown argus, having checked also the 
available list of species documented. So this was one! I chased this one for a few minutes, 
and got some better shots as my partner swatted away horseflies so I could hold the camera 
steady. I like this one of the three-quarter wing pose the best. A stem partially blocked the 
view, but I think that adds a bit of character.

<figure class="report-figure">

       alt="Northern brown argus">

Amazing. Species 43. But the horseflies were then so disruptive and we had to move on fast 
and by now, we felt had to get down the hill. A pair of mating green-veiled whites were also 
good to see. This place was seriously buzzing – the insect density just felt very high.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Myers_Allotment/Green_veined_whites.JPG"
       alt="Mating green-veined whites">

We followed the path back through some more woodland, horseflies not liking this part, them being 
confined mostly to the sunny open areas. I was still on fritillary hunt and to be honest something 
did dart from in front of me that I couldn’t fully rule out, but I suspect was a comma. It wasnt 
impossible for something interesting, but unlikely.

The strimmer person was closer and I could see clearer paths. We then passed by another entrance 
now being down near the roadside. This was a bigger one, probably the main in point, and was 
just a bit further up than the luscious one we entered in at. We probably should’ve come in at 
this spot. A bit further beyond this main gate looked inviting – another grassy open limestone 
area. There were at least two brown argus here too but they wouldn’t settle for a photo and were 
too far away. We took a look at our clocks, time to go. We had a long journey still ahead of us. 
We went back to the main entrance, exited back onto the road, and navigated back on the small 
roads back to the Leighton Moss car park. Off we then went back down south.

Overall, the Myers Allotment visit had been challenging, the vegetation, the horseflies, the 
circumstances of just having had a busy weekend, and the “sandwiched between fritillaries in 
the season” feeling. But, the site was alive, an amazing place, that had delivered a new species, 
the northern brown argus! And with good pictures too. I came away really pleased and looking back, 
had a general urge to revisit sometime, somehow in the future. High brown fritillary in 
particular will be a future target. Small pearl-bordered fritillary too would be nice and 
I read that conservation work is hoping to improve habitats for pearl-bordered fritillary 
and duke of Burgundy – for more information see here.
