### Day 1 11 July

Devil’s Dyke had got me hooked. A chalk site that harboured rarities was such a draw for me. 
I had already had great experiences with Adonis blue and dark green fritillary here. However, 
the site also held apparently good populations of another blue species, chalk hill blue.

This species flies a bit later in the season through July and into August. Things had already 
been very early this year and so I wasted no time in planning a ‘part three’ to my Devil’s Dyke 
journey to see the chalk hills hoping that they were emerging on schedule. Recent reports indeed 
had started coming in during the week ot two previous. The weather was predicted to be OK too, 
and this was shaping up to be a relaxed local jaunt.

Come the Saturday (11 July), the Newmarket entrance to Devil’s Dyke, by the July racecourse, 
was likely to be busy. The course had been hosting the Debenhams summer festival, the 11th 
being the final day. It may have been OK, but I didn’t fancy battling busier-than-usual traffic 
and access uncertainty. So, I changed tack. There are a few routes onto the Dyke, so I decided 
to go from the Burwell end. This was actually geographically closer to me, 20 mins away.

The weather again threatened to disrupt a bit. I was awake early, and at 6:30 the region was 
covered by a fairly solid cloud bank and suppressed temperatures about 17 degrees. This was 
different to the previous few days, but the clouds were scheduled to break about 9 am. That 
was fine. Come 9 am, I packed up my kit and headed out.

The sun was trying and succeeding to punch holes through the cloud. I arrived at the site with 
the sky improving all the time. I had seen on the map there was a small path by the side of the 
road to access the site. It was perfect. I geared up and headed down the entrance path.

This path had I think hawthorn bordering it, and there were a couple of browns here, meadow brown 
and gatekeeper. Quickly this path brings you out to Devil’s Dyke, and after a gentle incline, 
elevates you quite high up, possibly one of the highest parts on the escarpment.

more exposed shorter cropped grass, grazed by rabbits and livestock. It was here though I 
noticed something. I actually first thought it was a marbled white, but no, too small, and 
slightly blue. I thought it was too big at first to be chalk hill but actually no! A trick 
of the light it was a chalk hill blue! The slopes of the here were accessible with small 
paths, probably animal-made, cutting across allowing safe movement. I got a bit closer to 
this one and got my first pictures as it was keen to stay grounded.

It turned out that I had timed it well and had arrived to catch them as they basked, as the 
clouds parted and everything warmed up. Later on, they were far more flighty and were harder 
to picture, although still possible. I am glad to have had this early moment with them, and 
species number 46!
  
I moved further along the path as another itch needed scratching. On my previous visit 
to the site, I encountered dark green fritillaries in flight over the grasslands at the 
racecourse end. I had seen reports about 10 days previous, that someone had photographed 
one here. I was keen to see if I could as well to improve upon the in flight pictures I 
had already taken. This I realise was very optimistic, as they had probably been in their 
peak on 21st June, so by now that was 20 days after, and with a compressed maybe 4 to 6 week 
flight window, they could be all gone. For that, I wanted to scour the site to see if there 
was one still lingering.  

I therefore pressed on. The path, long and straight, hit several gates, and I passed sheep 
grazing directly on the slopes. I was scanning all flower and nectar sources. But birds also 
filled the air with song, lots of skylarks and yellowhammers in particular infiltrating from 
the surrounding farmlands.

## Day 2 12 July

It wasn’t the plan originally to turn this into a double header and go back to Devil’s Dyke the 
next day. But I had a nagging feeling about the Newmarket end, if this may harbour a lingering 
dark green fritillary or an early adonis blue. With the races now complete by the Sunday, I 
figured it would be easy just to go back and check. In fact here, I ended up finding neither 
today. But it is worth reliving this walk too as it was still a great day with lots going on.

The day started similar weather wise to the Saturday, but clouds parted earlier so I ended up 
getting out a bit earlier about a 9 am arrival. I climbed the first steps onto the small narrow 
path that made its way out onto the first slopes, where the adonis had been on the first visit.

The meadow though here on the chalky slopes had been transformed. There were lots of low growing 
white flowers and…dozens and dozens of chalk hill blues. They. Were. Everywhere. I had hundreds 
of pictures from yesterday but I could resist a few more.

longer here, and a reminder really how brief the adult flight period is for some species, 
and also how localised it is. A chat with a collegue later on revealed they had them at an 
elevated site in the North York Moors, well after my visit here.

I pressed on coming down and up the sections that allowed you to scoot off over to the racecourse

really nice still pockets. I ventured on and encountered an incoming light aircraft on the small
