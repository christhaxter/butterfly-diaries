## An early chance for Brown hairstreak at Beddington Farmlands, Greater London

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Beddington_Farmlands/Brown_hairstreak_3a.JPG"
       alt="Brown hairstreak">
</figure>

This trip was the most spontaneous of the season so far. At this stage in July, there were two 
species left on my list for this year, brown hairstreak and silver-spotted skipper. I had been 
reading more and more reports of both species emerging a bit early, and I had originally penciled 
in trips in August to try and pick them off. However, reports on Facebook of sightings in mid July 
had peaked my attention for brown hairstreak. One sighting in particular made me perk up and 
seriously consider a quick trip down to south London, the site, Beddington Farmlands.

A stunning video of a nectaring brown hairstreak had been posted the week prior. This was more 
than enough to tempt me. The location too had several things going for it. Specifically, it was 
accessible right near Hackbridge railway station. I thought I could pull it off as a quick survey 
and treat it as a low probability, high ease visit. A bit of conversation online suggested the 
exact location was at a specific hide on the site, so now this became interesting. A site in day 
travel range, a green trip (this was a major plus point) ditching the car, a known site for the 
species, with available young blackthorn, a specific location with nectar sources, with the species 
beginning to emerge – this was all aligning. Plus, we had plans anyway to go to London at some 
point to drop in at Limin’ for dinner before they closed in September.

Weather wise, it was looking good but uncertainty of when to go in the day, morning, evening 
was creeping in. The video posted was a fresh male in the evening. That seemed a good bet to 
target later in the day, but clouds were forecast to build before then. We therefore got an 
early train from Ely and arrived at Hackbridge and at the site around 10:45. This gave us ample 
chance to make a bit more of the morning period when the clouds were parting. This seemed ideal. 
It was still a bit cool early on, well, cooler than recent heatwaves, around 18 degrees on the 
way out, but it warming up fast.

I was a bit worried about my hip, that had been playing up, likely aggravated from the recent 
trips, and lots of walking. It turned out to be fine. After getting ourselves sorted, comfort 
breaks at the station, coffee in hand, we headed towards the site entrance. This was by a 
tiling shop, which then takes you across an overgrown walkway bridge.

<figure class="report-figure">

There was plenty of blackthorn here almost straightaway. You could see this on the train as it 
pulled into the station – the railway line runs alongside the site.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Beddington_Farmlands/Blackthorns.jpg"
       alt="Blackthorns aplenty">
  <figcaption>Blackthorns aplenty</figcaption>
</figure>

The site itself is south of Mitcham Common, and, once mostly agricultural, the few hundred acres 
here have since been transformed into a wetland area bordering the Beddington sewage plant, and 
has the makings of a great habitat. There are a series of bird hides, but the site was not yet 
fully completed so we could not access the whole. The paths near the railway however were open 
and this is where we needed to search anyway, where the blackthorn was thickest.

After the entrance bridge, we diverted off to hug the railway line and head to two of the bird 
hides. I was carefully scanning tops of blackthorn and any nectar sources, buddleia, knapweeds, 
brambles although many bramble flowers had gone to fruit.

Lots of times I was seeing potential interesting brown butterflies darting, some up at tree 
level, but many of these turned out to be meadow browns or gatekeepers, which were abundant. 
Speckled woods were also out and about, as were common blues.

The path continued and after about ten minutes we were approaching the bird hides overlooking 
the open expanse of the site.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Beddington_Farmlands/View_3.jpg"

A belt of mown grassland was present in that direction, and many flowers were present over there.

It was quite hot by now. We had further false alarms of browns, plus red admiral, painted lady 
too. And then, right by one of the hides, a jersey tiger moth appeared!
  
<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Beddington_Farmlands/Jersey_tiger.jpg"
       alt="Jersey tiger moth">

This was amazing, and totally threw me for a second, giving a flash of red and black, moving 
almost butterfly like. This one landed nicely for a pic, and was one of a few that we ended up 
seeing on the day.

The hides, not seated, had a bit of a breeze going through. We peered through the metal viewing 
slats and there was an amazing array of knapweeds, hemp, other grassland species on display, 
maybe cats ear or hawkweed too, and browns were everywhere, common blue too. My partner manned 
the rear of the spot we were at, while I peered out the front. This was right next to the young 
blackthorns, and there were many ash trees also nearby, maybe possible assembly points for mating 
and feeding brown hairstreaks. This location seemed like it had all the right ingredients.

After about 15 mins, a flash of yellow went by. There were whites fluttering around too but this 
was a clouded yellow!
  
<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Beddington_Farmlands/Clouded_yellow_1.JPG"
       alt="Clouded yellow">

They were distant and extremely flighty. I had previous form on this, with bad photography of 
one in Norfolk last year. This was as good as I got.

One of the issues here was that there was no option to get any closer to the flowers. Any pictures 
were therefore always going to be distant, unless the butterfly landed on flowers closer to the 
hide. It was around 11:30 by now and I was beginning to wonder if this was all a bit optimistic 
for brown hairstreaks. They can roam over large sections of blackthorn, and there was no guarantee 
any individuals would return to the exact same spot. However, we stayed a bit longer.

I kept scanning all of the flowers heads with binoculars; one knapweed kept tricking me, having 
a dried grasshead right in front that looked like a butterfly but wasn’t. My partner had been 
picking out other browns, but then called from behind me asking me what this one was. I came to 
the back of the hide and saw something flutter through, but I wasn’t sure – it was about 
gatekeeper sized and I seemed to move in front of the hide. I trained my bins on where it went 
with a rough idea of the area.

The image above shows the general view, a mass of greens and flowers. But then I did notice 
something. I got my bins up.

OMG, was that one?
  
Surely not.

It was!
  
This was unreal.

I took two seconds to check and admire it through the bins and tried to then memorise the exact 
spot. I wasted probably five to ten seconds not being able to find it on max 250 mm zoom on my 
camera. But eventually I got it.

It then went on top of the flower head. The grass stems were obstructive and at distance, I 
relied on heavy cropped images. But it got better as I got the close up setting on the Nikon 
Z50 working to try and focus on the tiny area I needed.

And then the blades of grass parted and I momentarily got the full butterfly, albeit soft focus.

<figure class="report-figure">

But there it was, species 47. This was remarkable, after an hour, at the spot that had been recommended.

Was this luck? Yes absolutely. However, the season had taught me a thing or two about patience, 
scanning and hairstreak tactics. I couldn’t be sure if this was what fluttered through the back 
of the hide to the flowers at the front, but it was likely.

It stayed only probably 30 to 40 seconds on that thistle head, and I saw it fly further down the 
cut grass ride, as I let out a ‘come back’ yelp. I saw no sign of it after that. The clouded 
yellows were still putting on a show, territorially bombing up and down the cut meadow area 
paths in front of the hide and into the distance. The meadow browns too still made their presence 
known.

I was elated, but of course I would love to have got a cleaner photograph. That motivated me to 
press on a bit further towards Mitcham Common to see maybe if we could find another, but I had 
no idea of densities here. Plus, we were very early in the season still, albeit with things heing 
ahead in the year.

A few other spots along the path still looked good. I spotted a location nearer the railway line 
containing loads of thistles. I crept in among the blackthorns and focused up at canopy level and 
down on the nectar. Is that one? Nope a common blue, but a good spot.

<figure class="report-figure">

We headed a bit further on but we had mistimed a bit, getting hungry, so we turned back and made 
a beeline for the shops we had past back at the main entrance to pick up some lunch. Taking a 
break in the shade at the stepped entry point was quite pleasant, nice and shaded. Neck flaps 
and sun visors had been deployed.

<figure class="report-figure">

We then headed back down the exact same route we had just used, still scanning nectar sources 
and the canopy.

We decided to stop at the other hide this time, and it was a nice spot for birds. There were 
lots of cormorants, tufted ducks, a few gadwall and lapwing here. A dabchick was also right by 
the hide! It then dived out of site. A reed warbler appeared and then flew left to right across 
the hide. A heron lumbered through the main island in the distance. Then a couple (of humans) 
turned up at the hide and we had a good chat about what was there. Apparently little ringed 
plover are here. I briefly slipped back into birding mode. Lots of gulls too were on the islands. 
After a brief chat we said goodbye and made our way on the path, about 13:30 by now.

We stopped again at our first hide, but no such luck this time for hairstreaks. In the distance 
we could hear a nearby racetrack with cars zipping by. Along the path, a baby dove was all I 
could see after yet another brown butterfly at height gave me the slip.

Further down the path, there were some good spots but devoid hairstreaks. This was beginning to 
seem like the sighting we had was a very lucky find. Lots of willowherb was around here.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Beddington_Farmlands/Another_spot.jpg"

We decided to take a left at a mini T-junction that headed back over the railway on a small 
bridge that took us onto part of Mitcham Common. We decided to head that way to get the train 
back from Mitcham station. This part of the Common is called the Gun Site, and has a few paths 
including one that curves around to the station.

I stopped quite a bit here, with brown argus, common blue and browns seen. Always things were 
flitting up to the canopy, and some were definitely browns, but a couple of the sightings looked 
quite hairstreaky to me. Several times I ended up following one and then losing it. I can see why 
they have such a reputation. I was probably a bit blasé having conquered white letter hairstreaks 
recently, but did I really think it would be that easy? Lesson relearned. Plenty of good 
blackthorns were still around here, and there were lots of gatekeepers, and a comma too.

We then reached the end of the Mitcham path, by about 14:30 bringing us out onto a main road 
near the station. Mitcham Common is much bigger than what we sampled and the noticeboard we 
came across made that clear.

<figure class="report-figure">

At the time, I was slightly crestfallen not to have had another good hairstreak encounter, 
but we had been here for a good few hours, about reaching our limit. To be honest looking back, 
any hairstreak encounter should be viewed as a triumph.

We then headed off back into town, getting off at Blackfriars and then walking across Millennium 
Bridge to Embankment and the South Bank for some early Limin’ dinner.

After that, we meandered off to Waterloo and home, after a tiring but hugely successful trip. 
I also felt quite pleased with the detective work I had put into scoping this place out. This 
also took me to a near clean sweep of hairstreaks this year except pesky green – I will get you 
next year! I was definitely going to try and chance it for brown hairstreak again at two other 
sites in the coming weeks to try my luck again.
