## Silver spots and hairstreak hopes in Oxfordshire

### Aston Rowant

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/AstonRowant_BernwoodMeadows/Silver_spotted_skipper_3.JPG"
       alt="Dark green fritillary">
</figure>

I had taken the week off work to get some downtime and to start to close out the 2026 
butterfly season in style. I had in mind a plan for the Tuesday to head off to Aston Rowant 
to complete the year’s butterfly ‘mission’. The target this time was the silver-spotted skipper. 
A joint venture with my other half, this was back in the car and a ca 3 h trip south, to hit the 
chalk meadows in the Oxfordshire Chilterns, being the nearest place we could find them.

Aston Rowant is a flower-rich chalk grassland, also with woodland and juniper scrub and boasts a 
rich community of butterflies. In high summer, the chalk hill and adonis blues are joined by the 
yellow silvery greenish skipper bullets as they dash around the nectar sources. This of course 
was a must see.

The weather was forecast as sunny and warm to hot, light winds. However, as usual (moan warning), 
the one day of the week I had picked was predicted to be the cloudiest.

Setting off at 8 am, the forecast felt like it was coming true, as a bank of grey stratocumulus 
type cloud had rolled in. Among the traffic stress, navigating a collision that brought things 
to a halt, we eventually reached our pre-site stop for a comfort break at Oxford Services. Here, 
the clouds by 11 am had parted leaving some very warm sunshine to punch through. Fifteen minutes 
away by this point on the M40, this was looking good and my spirits had lifted somewhat.

We had chosen to target the Cowleaze Wood side of Aston Rowant. The site itself is large with 
rolling hills and spans from Cowleaze and Bald Hill over to Beacon Hill. The Beacon Hill side 
looked great, and we even drove past the turn off for the official Aston Rowant turn off for 
Beacon Hill. We instead carried on in favour of Cowleaze, which is a Forestry Commission woodland. 
We were not visiting the wood itself, but using the car park as a convenience to then walk 
across to Aston Rowant NNR from there. The advice was to find the path that crosses the road 
and follow the signs leading back onto Aston Rowant NNR. The reason I was keen on this plan, 
was because others had posted that the skipper showing was strong on the Bald Hill side, so 
provided we could find it, I was confident in finding the skippers.

Parked up, fee paid, shoes on, coffee activated, we shuffled onwards to find the path back to 
Aston. I had printed out some maps of the site which helped understand the footpaths. At first, 
the path didn’t reveal itself after pacing the length of the narrow car park. However, the track 
was at the far end and it was super easy in the end, and well signposted.

Cowleaze looked wonderful too, and we passed familiar ferns and mixed deciduous trees as we 
headed away perpendicular to the wood crossing Christmas Common Road to Aston. Through a small 
gate we were greeted by a welcome sign, which always makes me smile, and makes you feel that you 
arrived at the right place.

familiarity only just the day before being amongst them. There were loads of common blues here 
fighting, flying, but no adonis surprisingly on Bald Hill, at least not where I was. The 
grassland just kept opening up as we walked.

scabious and in particular wild marjoram. The latter was very popular with butterflies. The 
grass sward was quite high too in places.

It did not take long before I spotted a yellowish darting blur, and shrieked in delight!
  
Yes they were right here!

A beautiful sliver-spotted skipper!

### Bernwood Meadows

After lunch, at about 1300, we left Cowleaze Wood to head over to Bernwood Meadows. This next 
site always crops up in conversation and online web searches for brown hairstreaks. It has 
good management, young and old Blackthorn thickets and is reportedly great for photos for 
nectaring individuals. We therefore said goodbye to Aston Rowant NNR and pit stopped again 
at the Oxford Services before heading then onto Bernwood Meadows.

The Bernwood meadow site is a traditional hay meadow complex managed by the Wildlife Trusts, 
and sits next to Bernwood Forest, managed by the Forestry Commission. The meadow is bordered 
by Blackthorn all the way around – it is immense in fact. A small car park of ca. three cars 
is available at the meadow entrance, and we got lucky as we pulled in with one other car there, 
able to slot in nicely. That was one uncertainty dealt with.

Out of the car we hopped. We headed through the small metal gate, which takes you out onto the 
meadow. A sign also greets you on arrival; note below the explicit mention of brown hairstreak 
and also black hairstreak (the latter earlier in the year of course).

The sun was hazy to full at this point, and when out in full, was very oppressive, pushing 
upwards of 30 degrees. A bit of wind took the edge off at times.

We made our way across the meadow eying some flower patches directly ahead. Meadow browns were 
aplenty.

I know I have said this in a few posts, but the flowers had largely gone over here. Bramble 
flowers were all but vanished, thistles and knapweed were mostly seed heads, although not quite 
all. We then saw a couple of chaps, one with a big telephoto camera lens. Ok this feels right, 
always a good sign when others are around doing the same.

We spoke to one of them. It turned out that they had the exact same itinerary as us, having come 
from Aston. It must be a common thing to do to pair these two sites together, being in such easy 
reach of one another. We struck up conversation about butterflies, yep we are here for the 
hairstreaks too, and so forth. He raved about Aston, as did I. I don’t know whether they were 
at the Beacon Hill side or not, perhaps likely though, as I hadn’t seen them over big Bald Hill. 
He mentioned all they had seen, which, also included adonis blue. We talked about hairstreaks, 
both agreeing the site here felt very dry, and hot but that they were here somewhere. It was 
their first visit here too, but they had also been over at Otmoor, the RSPB reserve (at some 
point recently) that also boasts a good population of brown hairstreaks; they had seen two 
tattered males at Otmoor, and so were looking for better pictures. I mentioned our jaunt to 
South London too where we had got lucky. Essentially, we were both there at Bernwood for the 
same reason, a better encounter.

We all made our way around the borders of the meadow, and so we had two sets of observers scanning 
the hedges, with an agreement for either of us to shout out if we got one.

I will not keep you hanging here, it was not to be this time. We did not see brown hairstreak 
at Bernwood, and I am 95% certain our new friends didn’t either. The 5% uncertainty is because 
we left before they did, but on viewing them in the near distance, body language, lack of yelps, 
and the fact they were just about at the end of the section of blackthorn near the car park 
having done a full circuit suggested it was likely that they had also come up dry like we did.

Our tactics were good though. We had a very thorough scan of the blackthorns, the tops, the 
nectar sources, of what little there was. We switched to a side of the field so as not to 
intersect our friends, and started out along the road-ward side of blackthorns. Here, several 
false positives pinged my radar, with many common darter dragonflies (I think), meadow browns, 
small heaths, the odd gatekeeper, and several common blues flitting at knee height to blackthorn
height.

At several stages, I was convinced I had a hairstreak, nope, meadow brown, nope small heath. On 
occasion there were some suspicious ones I didn’t ID, so it wasn’t a flat 100% ‘no’ on this 
visit, but one I consider improbable.

The meadow is actually two meadows joined by a small entrance to the second. As we made our 
way around the first of these, I was stopping every few paces, and spending most time by any 
thistles, knapweed, willowherb, meadowsweet and brambles that had any flowers left, scanning 
also intensely with binoculars. Several common blues were around, including fresh ones.

Making our way further around into the start of the edge of the second field anticlockwise,

Oh cool is that a….? Ah, a small copper!

About 14:30 by this point, some shaded patches of brambles birdering Bernwood forest were worth 
a pause at to scan the tops of a few remaining flowers with binoculars. But alas, not much was 
around, except meadow browns and common blues.

We arced our way around the blackthorn perimeter and bumped into to the chattier of the two 
guys again, having overtaken the other, still scanning one of the few flower patches; they 
were quite distant apart at this point having obviously decided to split up their effort. 
He still hadn’t seen anything, and he was interested in my small copper as he hadn’t seen 
one today. He noted a humingbird hawkmoth, that I then also spotted whilst he was talking. 
Parting company again, this was the last time we spoke, as he went to retrieve his partner, 
and we then wandered more briskly back to the car park direction on the opposite side of the 
second meadow. No more surprises though.

I briefly took a detour at the boundary of the two fields, leaving my partner to go back to 
the car, whilst I scanned one of the more promising thistle patches we had encountered in the 
first meadow. It was very hot by now but I had the feeling of wanting to give this my all and 
not to come away thinking, “if only I had just spent more time in area x or y….” and so forth. 
However, I saw nothing else of note, and so I headed back to the car.

On packing up, admittedly, I was frustrated. I didn’t want to leave like this, and it felt like 
a downer ending. It is very likely we had just timed it wrong, perhaps with the hairstreaks 
doing the sensible thing and staying still and hiding deep inside the blackthorns out of the 
hot sun. I took solice in the fact that we had given it a darn good go. Plus, two other 
experienced observers faced the same situation. To be honest, deep down I knew this was an 
opportunistic and low probability visit, with only about 90 mins spent on site, 15:15 by now 
as we were leaving. Brown hairstreaks are notoriously difficult that can evade even the best 
of prying eyes. After all, we had seen one at Beddington two weeks ago, and that’s just the 
way butterflying goes sometimes.

We then headed home remembering the joy that the silver-spotted skippers had provided us back 
at Aston Rowant.
