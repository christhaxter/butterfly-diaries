## Back with the blues at Devil's Dyke, Suffolk

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_07_27/Chalk_hill_blue_4.jpg"
       alt="Dark green fritillary">

Off we go again. The urge to take a stroll back at Devil's Dyke was strong and continued to keep me 
gripped. My partner and I had been on a weekend away visiting Twycross Zoo the days before and having fun with our 
nephew and nieces. That had left us tired, but satisfied. We had also visited the butterfly farm 
part of Twycross, which was quite amazing and a somewhat different experience of the butterflies
out in the wild. Where’s that species? Oh just look up, there are ten of them! 
Not quite typical of reality, except maybe chalk hill blues if you look down! 
It was still amazing though.

This Twycross visit has nothing to do with Devil’s Dyke, but it set the scene for a week off 
work that I had coming up. Obviously butterflies were occupying much of my planning.

The Monday came around and although tired, I had a lazy start and got out to the Newmarket 
side about 11:40. The sky was full of cloud before I left but the temperatures were warm and 
I was determined just to get out and about.

I made my way across the first slope; that is where the above pics were taken. A few meadow 
browns, the odd gatekeeper, less than previously for sure, but a fair few small heaths were 
around.

Another reason for returning to this site, not just because it is local and easy 
to reach, was to see if any second brood adonis blue were out. I came across a lot of common 
blues. However, no such luck this time finding an adonis, but there’s still time in the season.

<figure class="report-figure">

I flushed a tatty looking red fox from across the other side – that was rather unexpected. 
I passed a couple of gents then coming the other way, in jovial chatter. One said said hello, 
and then he clocked that I was a bit of a shutter bug; “looking for blues?” he asked. “There’s 
loads back that way”, gesturing back in the direction he had come. It was great the chalk hills 
were putting on a show.

Walking further down, overlooking the racecourse stands, I ended up chasing a green woodpecker, 
pretty much the whole time up and down the walk on the chalk.

I made my way further along the second bank, reaching the mid section of the embankment that 
allows an easy transition onto the July course. I didn’t do that, but instead milled around the 
shrubs there. A moth showed well that I presumed was a lattice heath at the time but actually the 
IDs suggested this was more likely a common heath. Nice.

About 12:50 by now it was getting hot in the upper 20s [degrees C] as the sun had gradually come 
out more. It was also quite windy too on the top.

I decided to drop down out of the wind, where I had done well for common blues before where the 
habitat becomes more generic meadow in the transition to the course. At least four or five common 
blues were in this same spot and a brown argus too.

I noticed a couple of folks walking on the Newmarket course coming my way. I diverted back up on 
the top, where the green woodpecker flushed again. Idling along, I startled as a big black dog 
barked and growled charging over to me. Black shuck maybe? It kept stopping, then barking and 
advancing. I froze, relaxed and stood in a direct open position looking right at it. My heart 
was beating. I don’t really have a fear of dogs but I defy anyone in this situation not to have 
been just a bit on edge. The dog belonged to the two walkers. My tactics worked as the dog’s 
charge and suprise was not working and it was then unsure of me. I have been bitten in the past, 
so I was pleased that this turned out ok. The owners dealt with the situation and we parted ways.

Moving on, a brown argus raised my spirits, and the chalk hills continued to flutter around.

I then spied two other folks on the side of the slope thst I was on coming my way. One had a 
clipboard clearly surveying the butterflies, and another was assisting. They were doing an 
official survey. This was great. I passed them eventually as they slowly moved by. I felt guilty 
that I too was not being a bit more thorough with my records, as I tend to go for a casual 
general estimated count submitted to irecord rather than a precise number. This made me think 
though, was I really correct in say, 100+ chalk hills? On this first section, or over the 
length I walked? I pondered this as I made my way back up the slope.

Back on the top path, leading back through the first narrow corridor of hawthorns, blackthorns 
and other scrub, I noticed a beetle on the path in front of me.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_07_27/Bloody_nosed_beetle.jpg"
       alt="Bloody-nosed beetle">

After looking this up, it turns out this was a bloody-nosed beetle. This beetle emits a red ooze 
from it’s mouth when threatened, and is apparently seen crossing paths like this. Interesting, 
and I do not recall having seen this one before.

I made my way back to the car, had some lunch, water and pondered the day’s events, the dog, the 
surveyors etc. I actually then couldn’t resist another quick look to estimate a bit more 
accurately how many chalk hill blues were out on that first meadow slope.

I got back on the top and started counting. The numbers racheted up quickly. I could still see 
the two surveyors right in the distance at the end of the first bank stretch now. I was 
obviously not doing this as thoroughly as they were, and I was going faster than their walk, 
stop, write tactics, as is the standard 5 m imaginary box approach, in which butterflies around 
you are counted. I was reminded in fact of the Tottenhoe chap that we had come across who maybe 
did something similar. My final tally was around 95 to 100 on that first meadow stretch before 
the path drops down and back up again onto the second stretch. This line transect faux survey 
reassured me that my estimates were likely not massively out at least.

I then called it a day and walked back briskly to the car, but I have a feeling it probably 
won’t be the last time I am here this year.
