## Devil’s Dyke (the fifth): A search for a different shade of blue turned to copper

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_01/Small_heath.JPG"
       alt="Small heath">
</figure>

Devil's Dyke had by now become a local patch, and who can blame me. It is has a kind of draw to it,
and an intrigue - with the commanding views over the flat landscape and the buffet of wildlife on 
display. Technically this was the *sixth* visit to the site but I am treating as number five as 
a weekend double visit (11-12 July) I am treating as one, not that it matters! Anyway, I again 
set off in an opportune moment on a Saturday at the back end of 
my week off work to have a gentle stroll again heading in from the Newmarket end – I will have 
to try the Burwell side again and other parts along the route. 

The weather was quite warm, high 20s, winds not too bad, clouding over by lunchtime, so I 
started out at around 9:00. I had no main targets in mind, except I confess I was half hoping 
still for a vivid blue flash to appear from a male second brood Adonis blue! Can always hope, but
who needs an excuse to get out for a nice walk.

The escapades of Thursday this same week at Bookham Commons where I failed at brown hairstreak 
was still lodged firmly in my mind. I needed a visit like this just to clear the air, to walk 
and enjoy nature, without feeling a pressure to see something for a change. To be honest, all 
visits should probably be like that.
  
There was another reason for an early start this day – it was race day at Newmarket. On a previous 
visit for chalk hill blue, I shied away from targeting Newmarket on race day for fear of a road 
closure or trickier visiting circumstances. With no stakes on this visit, I wanted to see what 
things looked like at my usual starting point. Indeed there were cordons and traffic cones blocking 
usual parking spots, except a few, and the main green next to the road was already open for cars 
visiting the races – gates opened officially at 11:30, so I figured a good two hours beforehand 
would be fine. And in fact it turned out to be ok; I was early enough to miss the masses.

Up on the top of Devil’s Dyke, it was lovely, quiet except the preparations taking place across 
from me for the races.

A red kite was a good start. And in the familiar first embankment section, straight away, the 
chalk hill blues were busy. Basking, flying, nectaring, not too warm so they dashed in a frenzy, 
and not too cool so they stayed grounded. I had so many pictures of them already from previous 
visits here, but they are irresistible.

Some were looking a little more worn now, and there were fewer of them, although still very 
abundant, and at times, I still had to watch my step to give them space on the paths – I mainly 
stuck to one of the paths than ran along the slope rather than on the crest of the ridge.

I was on a bit of a mission to play close attention to the other blues, that were all common 
blues, no Adonis. The one below had me going for a bit, but nope.

thistles, providing support for whites, common blues and a wonderful hummingbird hawk moth.

brown argus? I was transported back to Bookham Common and brown hairstreak misadventures. But no. 
This was another small copper!

They had been turning up quite a bit now at the sites I had visited, this was site number three 
for me where I had seen one. It was the first time I had seen one here at Devil’s Dyke. But what 
a stunner.

I had also seen quite a few small heaths, and in fact more than both meadow browns and gatekeepers 
combined, that had both massively dropped off since last time.

I glanced at my phone being now around 10:30 – time had flown and I was perhaps half thinking of 
not leaving it too much longer here to beat the incoming race crowds. Time still just to pop onto 
the next embankment. Dropping down and up again, I noticed of course the entrance to the race 
course was closed. Up on the second stretch, I was feeling perhaps this was not the time for a 
massive hike. Instead I turned around to focus back on the first section.

Back on the home stretch, 11:05 now, I paused at some other thistles on the other side of the path 
opposite my usual slope. I perhaps don’t look enough that way but here, a nice green-veined white 
was there, plus other common blues. I stood there for a good ten minutes just enjoying things. 
I was also taking in the general habitat and admiring the flora, such as bird's foot trefoil.

Then a dash of blue caught my eye among the chalk hills. I was getting better at 
spotting the common blues among the chalk hills, being smaller, and with different flight. 
This one was flying a bit faster still. However, not to get hopes up, it was a bright common blue.

It was about time to leave. I could see across to the car park starting to get busier as 11:30 
approached. Another tattered female chalk hill blue came into sight.

I did have a feeling that this season was ending and whilst I felt a general sadness, there 
had been some great times this year, and this was not the end. I will have time to squeeze in 
other visits, although I was already excited for next year. I sat down on a slope for a few 
minutes just to take it in, sitting amongst the chalk hills, letting them come to me for a change. 
But, time to go.

I headed back down the narrow entrance path away from the first slope on the way out. But 
just as I was exiting, I noticed a small little gap in the side vegetation, which led back 
onto the slope. I peered through and this was literally the very start of the chalk escarpment, 
the first point you could get onto it and I had not seen that before. I spotted then another 
chalk hill blue just nectaring here. Technically that was the closest one I had recorded to the 
entrance.
