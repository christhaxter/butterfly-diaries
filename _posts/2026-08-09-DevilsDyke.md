## Third time's a charm in chasing blues at Devil's Dyke

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_09/Adonis_blue_cover.JPG"
       alt="Adonis blue">
</figure>

Another day and another visit back to Devil's Dyke. I headed out again to the Newmarket end for a 
gentle stroll after a lovely busy Saturday with family. After having seen Adonis blue
here earlier in the year, again my attention had turned, rather obsessively, to the potential 
for an Adonis blue second brood to be emerging soon. Could this be the day?

I arrived around 9:45, with it predicted to be warm up to nearly 30 degrees later on. This was 
now developing into the fifth heatwave of the year. Although earlier in the season I fretted due 
to rainfall at Monkwood, that seemed a very long time ago. We haven’t had any decent rainfall in 
weeks, rendering much of southern England under drought conditions.

I made my way up to the top and was greeted with the usual familiar sight.

A few folks were on the route today, groups of friends, various walkers and joggers, taking 
advantage of the nice day.

At first, on the initial slopes of the first embankment, I was struck by just how quiet it was 
for insect life. It took me a good five minutes before I even saw a butterfly. Perhaps it was 
still early in the day, and butterflies do enjoy a lie in. I was beginning to feel saddened – 
had all the chalk hill blues dropped off that quickly since my last visit? Then I saw a lone male 
drifting over the slope. And then another. They were still here, just much fewer in number. I also 
saw some females, territorial battles, and matings further along. Ok fine, that was more in line 
with what I had expected.

The swallows were amazing too flying over the slopes as autumn departures approached. I moved 
further along and I was starting to see a couple of small heaths flying over the slopes and 
across the top.

Regarding Adonis blues, I was not overly hopeful at this point. I figured with recent failures 
and duck-outs at Aston, and here twice now for a second brood, that I may be too early still. 
I hadn’t actually seen a common blue yet up to this point, until I made my way diagonally down 
in the direction of the nectaring spots at the end of the first embankment, now very much gone over
for flowers, but not completely.

A lonely gatekeeper dashed by here but that was the only one I saw. Meadow browns too were more 
scarce than previously, doing their best to hide in the grasses.

<figure class="report-figure">

I then caught sight of what at first I thought could be a brown argus, but which turned out to 
be a small copper.

This was the first of a few I came across all the way along. I lingered a bit here watching the 
whites drifting by, likely small whites. Chalk hills kept coming and going too and a tattered 
common blue dropped in.

<figure class="report-figure">

I was quite near the bottom of the valley here. Moving back up to the top and traversing the 
small walkway I switched to the second escarpment section.

Making my way further along, it was a little quiet but a few chalk hills and small heaths were 
highlights. Then it was up and down onto the third embankment, where the dark green fritillaries 
were most abundant back in June. I also flushed a white wagtail juvenile here.

I then also disturbed a green woodpecker, as I tend to each time now I come here. One of many 
micro moths that I always seem to scare out of the grasses I at last got a look at, which was 
likely a grass veneer moth spp.

<figure class="report-figure">

I then decided to push on a bit further and was now at the end of the third embankment, more or 
less opposite a small light aircraft runway across the other side of the racecourse. A plane 
landed and took off whilst I was there. This area becomes flatter, grassier, shrubier too, I 
think with buckthorn, and quite a few thistles and other plants were in flower. Also yarrow 
was growing quite prolific out on the actual racecourse to my right. This felt like a good spot. 
Chalk hills breezed by but were not numerous here. Some stopped to nectar on flowers like small 
scabious.

And then, another common blue, or at least that’s was I thought…. This one didn’t immediately 
scream a vibrant blue in flight but it was sunny here adding glare, and was now quite hot. But 
the blue I saw definitely had a darker/deeper tinge to it. I thought I should check it out, but 
I had squared this away in my mind, almost certainly a vibrant common blue.

It landed first on some sainfoin, also growing well here. It then stopped to nectar on some 
(I think) saxifrage.

It was an adonis blue!!

It stubbornly refused to open its wings by more than 4%. The chocolate 
brown underside was free of grey tinges and was too small to be chalk hill, plus I knew already 
this was a male with the blue I’d seen. The spot mis-alignment on the hind wing was present, but 
more than anything, those fringes clinched it. It was undeniably adonis. The next one shows a 
slither of the blue.

chased several other male blues (that were not chalk hills) that were also flying around. Of 
course there is a chance one ‘could’ve’ been another adonis, some being brighter, but every 
blue I got eyes on one, it turned out to be common blue.

What a bevvy of species! I still couldn’t believe the Adonis had showed. I hung around a bit 
longer but by 11:40 it was now getting quite warm. I left it there, not seeing the adonis again, 
and briskly headed back. I made sure to do a better count of the number of chalk hills too than 
I did on the way out. I tallied about 40 to 50 in the end. A red admiral, painted lady and 
hummingbird hawkmoth also flew by (not all at the same time!).

I was keen however to get back and examine the images. I had quite the grin on my face though 
on the return leg.

When I got home, I reflected on the day. One thing in particular, this felt like something of a 
redemption. The last visits here were mostly just walks, taking a stroll on the hillside, 
low-stakes trips, but in the back of my mind I had been always secretly hoping for something here. 
Also the Bookham trip, missing brown hairstreak by a whisker, on top of not seeing them at 
Bernwood Meadows, still stuck in my mind a bit. So this find of an adonis felt really good. 
This was a second brood of course being at this stage in August. I hadn’t seen any posts 
suggesting them at Devil’s Dyke at this time, but I am sure there were some I hadn’t come across.

I wasn’t sure if this Devil’s Dyke trip would be my last this season on arrival today, but 
by the time I left, that had totally flipped – I was very keen to come back again to check 
for Adonis blues, and whatever else I could find.
