## In search of royalty at Ringstead Downs

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Ringstead_Downs/Clouded_yellow_5.jpg"
       alt="Clouded yellow">
</figure>

This trip was a last minute decision to try something a bit different. I am not really one that 
sets off on long journeys to ‘twitch’, but certainly will do if is local. That is kind of what 
happened here. I saw the reports through BirdGuides and Facebook, of the influx of 
Queen of Spain fritillary (hereafter QOSF to save words!) in the south – some had even been seen 
in Norfolk, and at a site an hour away from me, nearer the north Norfolk coast. Clouded yellow 
were also now widely being reported. Now that was well worth a look!
  
I couldn’t get there unfortunately when the bulk of the Norfolk sightings happened, with the 
last one recorded the 11th August, four days ago from this visit. I was a bit late to the party. 
It was highly unlikely one was still around, but I figured why not have a jaunt out at a new site 
anyway and enjoy the habitat. I went with low expectations.

On this day, there had still been sightings further south in Hampshire and Sussex on the previous 
day. Some long-tailed blue sightings were also coming in too further south. I had seen 
long-tailed blue in Southern France last year, but, that would be cool!

<figure class="report-figure">

This was all a bit pipe dream territory of course, but after having rare things turn up in my own 
garden, like a dark-eyed junco (!), it shifts your perspective somewhat.

Ringstead Downs, managed by Norfold Wildlife Trusts, is a large expanse of chalk grassland, and 
as stated by the Trusts, is a glimpse back as to how the countryside looked in the past. It was 
well worth a visit anyway regardless of anything rare flying around.

The Saturday was the day after the fifth UK heatwave had ended. The days before had been up to 
38 deg C in some places in the UK. The country was on high heat vigilance wildfire alerts, as a 
drought had taken hold. The Saturday however was a drop back down to around 20 degrees. In East 
Anglia, cloud had rolled in overnight. The best window for butterflies I thought was in the 
afternoon on Saturday, where to the north near Hunstanton, a big break in the clouds was predicted. 
I aimed for that.

Heading over I arrived at the site around 14:30, good timing, following the edge of the cloud bank 
on the way, as the sun came out just in time.

The site looked decent, and easy, a good sized car park that wasn’t too busy, perhaps not a good 
sign if everyone knows the fritillary has likely gone. However, at least some people were here.

The reports had placed the QOSF on the track at the car park. I searched here first, but no luck. 
Just imagine that, seeing it here, it must’ve been such an adrenaline rush (spoiler alert: it 
wasn’t here today, I don't think...). This path was next to a very dry meadow with a few 
remaining knapweed and other nectar sources.

After enough stalling around the car park, I figured anything as mobile as a QOSF may have moved 
on through. There were also reports of more than one apparently, so perhaps another may be lurking 
further on the reserve. Yeah, right. But any excuse for a walk!
  
I pushed on down a track that led to the entrance to the Downs.

That signpost in the picture above directs you either left around an old chalk pit, or right to 
the Downs proper. I wasn’t sure which way to go at first, or if the chalk pit was the place to 
be for traditional chalk fauna. This was starting to sound like the Matrix film 
(for film buffs) – I took the left path to stay in wonderland.

This pit is now heavily wooded. Passing through the wooded path brings you into a clearing, 
with an amphitheatre vibe, with picnic benches, here being at the base of the large chalk wall.

<figure class="report-figure">

There was little in the way of serious butterfly activity here. Not knowing the area, I 
wondered if I had to get somehow higher. Other people were also around here and I passed a 
family, some kids in the woods too. I looked around to the right just to check out the woodland, 
coming across a pair of speckled woods dancing in a frenzy in front of me (no pic though).

The track went generally downwards, and then joined up with the other path that turned right 
avoiding the wood. So now I was on the Downs path.

Here, it was nice, bordered by wood on either side and sheltered out of the sun, which although 
not as hot as days gone by, was still quite warm in the full sun. The path cut through the meadow 
grasses.

I passed a some other folks mainly couples and dog walkers. Near the end of this stretch the 
path opens out again leaving woods behind. And just before here a butterfly was on the path, 
medium sized, in dappled sun distracted my view of colour, but I flushed it high and headed 
canopy height.

Oh what was that!
  
I will never know, but it could have just been a flighty meadow brown, or a painted lady more. 
However, it reminded me almost of a purple emperor that just kept climbing, heading skyward 
for an oak tree. Enough to ponder, and a ‘what if’ moment for QOSF. QOSF are powerful fliers 
and one could argue that a painted lady may not have behaved that way. Also – and this was 
only found out later on irecord – all along this stretch of the path QOSF had been sighted 
in the days prior. That is very interesting, and it still stuck in my mind as to whether on 
balance, it could have been a QOSF! I hung around but nothing showed.

Another small wooded part then approaches as you pass through another gate, into the next section. 
Here I bumped into a chap with binoculars – I briefly asked if anything had been seen, nah, it was 
a bit quiet.

Passing into the next meadow, this habitat really started to buzz with activity. The knapweed was 
amazing all through to the end of the reserve from this point.

I was expecting that.

I then crossed paths with them. The couple asked me if I knew butterflies. “Yeah, a bit”, I said.

shuffled along from the car park to this section. I caught them up. “Are you into butterflies” 
they asked. “Yeah, you’re not here for the QOSF by any chance” I replied. That sparked a chuckle 
as we were both on the same page. We then talked about the location, the chances of seeing the 
queen, being five days ago, the car park track where it was seen and so on. We both had the same 
feeling like it was probably a wild goose chase, or an equivalent butterfly pun. We left it at 
that and I let them get back enjoying the brown arguses, small heaths and other species. It was 
good to see folks doing what I do, taking pictures of the more common stuff. It’s not all about 
the rare things after all.

I glanced at the time. It was around 16:45 ish by now and the time had flown by. My partner 
and I were meeting in Ely for dinner so I had to get a bit of a move on. Passing back on the 
forked path for the chalk pit, I was now back at the small track leading back to the main car 
park. Here, a comma was just basking! I remember feeling glad that I didn’t see that flash of 
orange in flight, as my heart would’ve skipped a few beats given a potential QOSF.

sightings persisted down in Hampshire. So, yes I probably (!) missed it (although there was 
a large lingering doubt about that path butterfly that could well have been one). However, 
the clouded yellow consolation prize was more than enough to make up for a near miss. Plus, 
what an amazing walk this had been though through the Norfolk countryside.

## Appendix (FOMO)

The QOSF continued to be reported down in Hampshire up to around 25 August, at Monk Sherbourne 
north of Basingstoke. This was quite amazing and the reports seemed to indicate some fresh-looking 
individuals, maybe suggesting an emergence of a local UK homegrown population.

I had a massive urge to twitch them in Hampshire and FOMO, but then this was offset with green 
guilt, and general logistics. I kind of set a rule that I will only chance a real rarity of the 
transient kind, if it is more ‘local’ and feasible, like Ringstead here in this post. Hampshire 
would have been a 3.5 h+ journey each way, and in this case was not quite as easy to achieve by 
being greener on the trains.

Part of me felt hypocritical. I have set off on some longer excursions. However, for the good of 
conservation. I was trying therefore to balance a few variables – the MASSIVE urge I had after 
seeing Facebook reports to go for it, vs the hobby overall, and general logistics. I am sure we 
all wrestle with these aspects.

However…it turns out that I got another bite at the cherry anyway, as an opportunity then arose 
down in Essex – see my later post on that.
