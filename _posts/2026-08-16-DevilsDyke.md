## Another dance with the Devil in pursuit of blue

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/Adonis_blue_7.JPG"
       alt="Adonis blue">
</figure>

After the day before spent at Ringstead Downs, I couldn’t resist a solo trip back at the place I 
had come to love, to Devil’s Dyke. This was primarily to see if that Adonis blue I had seen a 
week ago was just a lucky find, or still there with others, and if indeed a second brood of 
Adonis was underway.

The weather was around the low to mid 20°C, and had been billed as cloudier in the morning, 
improving with sunny spells, so I was targeting the afternoon.

We had been chopping back our lavender hedge in the morning outside our house, but it was more 
sunny than expected. I was keen to down tools and get out to the site soon to take advantage of 
the conditions.

I got out promptly at around 14:00.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/View_1.jpg"

It was quite nice at first, but the clouds had started to build again, and most of the time I 
was out here it was quite cloudy, with a moderate breeze over the tops.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/Clouds.jpg"

Out onto the slopes, it was very quiet, much quieter than even last time. The chalk hill blues 
were much reduced. It took a few minutes to see one, and there were nearly all females.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/Chalk_hill_blue_1.JPG"

That is to be expected with females emerging perhaps a bit later than males.

There were not that many though, prob about 6 to 10 per section, perhaps a few more at times, 
but nothing like the 100+ previously. And I only saw three males the entire time I was there.

I had taken my usual route dropping diagonally down at the end of the first embankment down to 
the thistle patch I had favoured on previous weeks, now very gone over.

<figure class="report-figure">

<figure class="report-figure">

Scrambling back up again and onto the second embankment, again as last time, another small 
copper showed, as well as a hummingbird hawkmoth buzzing around the slopes looking for 
nectar – I must’ve seen three or four small coppers on the day.

<figure class="report-figure">

The female chalk hills were keeping me amused. I flushed them a fair bit but sometimes they 
just plonked themselves right on the path for some great views.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/Chalk_hill_blue_4.JPG"
       alt="Chalk hill on the chalk">

I spooked a kestrel that was sat in a tree on the right-hand side of the track, a nice male.
Then very soon after this, an amazing sighting, a flash of yellow…

A clouded yellow!
  
💛

I learned later that some other folks had seen them at Devil’s Dyke recently as well. It has 
been a good year for clouded yellows. I tracked it back the way I had come for a few paces, 
as it had approached me and passed back behind me. However, there was no chance of it stopping 
and therefore no pic. That was in complete contrast to the ones yesterday I had at Ringstead 
Downs, but was more in keeping with their flighty character I was used to. In fact, I was 
fortunate to see the yellow at all. I periodically dip down and peer down into the slopes and 
valley before popping back to the top path. I just chose to do that at the exact moment when 
it breezed by.

I pushed on.

I was keen to get back to the spot where I had seen Adonis blue last weekend. This was the 
flatter section after the end of the third embankment.

I moved a bit quicker to try and mop up any remaining sunshine. On arrival, it was quiet. 
I moved down the path a little, and there were some blues around. I tried to track as many 
as I could to see if any may be an Adonis.

Some kids had also moved through this same section, and a group also on the racecourse 
playing football. Nice to see others about. I spent nearly an hour, from about 15:00 to 
16:00, pacing up and down, but I had to face facts that it wasn’t happening here.

I decided to try and see what the fourth embankment was like, having not often got out 
that far. It was a steep and decent section, with quite a lot of scabious too, and deep 
grasses. Chalk hills again appeared on the path – that chalk must be nice at warm and a 
little microclimate for them.

a bonus chalk hill blue also sharing that spot.

I could also see the end of the Newmarket section in the distance from here, nearer the A14, 
probably where I had walked to from the Burwell end on one of the previous visits to Devil’s 
Dyke. I’ve still never quite fully joined up the dots though walking all the way.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/View_3.jpg"

Feeling slightly tired after yesterday at Ringstead Downs, and gardening in the morning today, 
I started to head back.

A group of gulls caught my eye off to the side in the racecourse, which I think were mainly 
lesser black-backed and herring gulls, although I didn’t have time to check fully for anything 
more interesting lurking among them. Some immatures too were mixed in.

<figure class="report-figure">

Back by the flat meadow section now after leaving the fourth embankment, I came across more 
common blues again, some very smart ones too.

<figure class="report-figure">

I decided to leave it there though. Fair enough. I had given it a darn good go. The Adonis 
must be very few in number, or amazing at keeping hidden here. Or maybe last time that was super 
lucky and an unusual spot to find them. I was still learning the site and how it works really.

Moving quicker now to get back, the female chalk hills kept landing obligingly.

Look, you are very cute but I have to leave now.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/Chalk_hill_blue_7.JPG"

Before I knew it I was back at the first embankment. Here we are again I thought.

I was actually reminiscing of the moment I had first come to Devil’s Dyke and how I had dipped 
down of the top only to find an Adonis here at the last minute. I was thinking how lucky that 
was and had remembered the circumstances and the way that had happened, obviously not expecting 
it again, but more just out of nostalgia.

I walked a bit along, stopped, scanned, looked down the slope, dropped down a bit to see what was 
lower, went back up again to the top, and then repeated that. That was the previous tactic.

I also tried to remember the exact spot back in June. I was in the middle of the first section 
by this point.

Then, it becomes a bit of a fairy tale.

Can you guess what happened next?
  
This was a bewildering moment. I saw a flash of blue. OK, probably another common blue, but 
it was bright. I had been fooled here recently before though.

The butterfly stopped.

I raised my camera.

Boom. ADONIS!
  
<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/Adonis_blue_1.jpg"
       alt="Adonis blue, makes a late appearance!">

This was incredible. It was like time was repeating itself – had I just time-slipped?
  
Again, I was about to head back, given up, and there it was. This was the third time really 
if you count the first adonis, and then the black hairstreak at Fermyn Woods. And what was 
also interesting here, was the circumstances were almost exactly the same as the first adonis 
sighting. The time of day: later afternoon. The conditions: cloudier, and a bit breezy. 
Coincidence? Well it was only a couple of data points so I couldn’t read too much into it, 
but your mind goes there.

I shuffled down the slope to get closer.

It was another stubborn one keeping its blue a secret. I then pushed too hard and spooked it.

Oh oh, had I blown it?
  
I followed it really easily. It’s blue stood out well against the slope, and then I caught up 
with it again as it landed on a flower head.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_16/Adonis_blue_6.JPG"

Videos were taken too. He was letting me get close and was looking amazingly fresh. I stuck a 
little while longer here sat in the slope next to the adonis. Some people across the other 
side of the ditch across on the road looked over at me as if to say, what on earth is that 
guy doing? I wasn’t concerned, I had waited for this moment.

The question – would he reveal his blue?
  
Well, yes!! But only for about 5 seconds.

Grass stem in the way, but wow! That was all I needed. He flitted once more to another flower 
head and was actually defending the patches here against other occasional chalk hill blues.

Stunning. I then lost sight of him and I made my way back, around 16:50 by this point. That 
was probably a good 20 mins or more I had spent with the adonis blue. You can’t ask for more 
than that. I had begun to wonder where the female adonis blues may be, as I had not come 
across any, with every brown blue butterfly that I had seen being a chalk hill. I am sure 
they are around somewhere.

It had been an amazing day and the trip felt like a closed loop from my first visit. It is 
funny how these trips write their own scripts. The manner of this find was again another 
incredible moment from a fun packed season.

Would I be back again this year? Not sure. I was actually starting to get a bit exited about 
next year too. I first came here at the tail end of the adonis brood number 1 in June; I had 
seen posts of up to 20 or more adonis blues at their peak in May, which must’ve been amazing 
to see. However, by then I had also missed green hairstreak and dingy skipper, where I had 
instead enjoyed the latter at Totternhoe this year. I will have to explore more of the Reach end 
of Devil’s Dyke too next year. But what a great way to have ended this visit, and with lots still 
to look forward to.
