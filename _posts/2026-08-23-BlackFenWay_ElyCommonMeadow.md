## Back to my patch: Black Fen Way and Ely Common Meadow

### Black Fen Way

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Ely_Common_Meadow/2026_08_23/Common_blue_2.JPG"
       alt="Common blue">
</figure>

This is my traditional local patch. After the week spent contemplating a twitch down in 
Hampshire for Queen of Spain Fritillary, I decided very much against that plan, and instead chose 
to head out on foot to a splendid local walk. This route takes you from Ely/Queen Adelaide over 
to towards the railway lines and river beyond, taking in meadow and arable fen habitats. After 
following the river Great Ouse, the path then loops back on itself over the fen fields to where 
it started, or with an option to go through Queen Adelaide and over to Roswell Pits. I chose a 
combination route today, and essentially split the hike into two (or three!) parts. So, Black Fen 
Way first.

Starting out very local, even before reaching the Black Fen route, the view over the local green 
by the back of the houses was parched from the recent heatwaves. About 10:50, I was a bit late 
setting off. Today was not that hot, very pleasant, about 22 degrees, very sunny with some clouds 
building later, and light winds.

This route is part of a larger trail called the Black Fen Waterways Trail and takes in part of 
the larger Fen Rivers Way network (I hope I am getting these names right). The Black Fen route 
can also link up Ely to Chettisham, but I did not take that trail. There are a network of paths 
to take and you can lose yourself along the river stretches, helping to de-stress with a good 
dose of vitimin G. Whatever the route, it is all good, and takes you over some proper fen soil.

The path here (in the image above) runs between fields, one of which is being developed for houses 
now, and also has a nice small reed bed section to the left as you head out. I’ve heard water rail 
and cuckoo there before. Trees line a drainage ditch, including birch, elms, oak and sycamore. 
The habitat is typical meadow (unsure of the specific NVC habitat type) with plenty of burdock, 
hogweed, thistles, ragwort and plenty of phragmites reeds lining the ditches. Today, it was 
pretty quiet – in spring there are chiffchaff, whitethroat, lesser whitethroat, blackcap, 
reed warbler and cettis warbler in full song. However, most of these species showed eventually 
today with calls (except lesser whitethroat). A reed warbler was feeding young in the trees, a 
blackcap alarmed, and linnets also twittered overhead. A green woodpecker yaffled in the 
distance, and a stock dove cooed. Pretty decent. Small whites were also aplenty, as well as 
some green-veined whites.

Glorious weather here, and quite hot in the sun, reed warblers and sedge warblers were chirping. 
Small heaths continued to appear occasionally, but it was quite quiet up here on the top of the 
bank for butterfly life. And of course, all the way along the walk, I was not thinking at all 
about if there would be a wandering fritillary ;)!
  
I had detected some common terns from before reaching the river, and now here they were milling 
overhead and moving along the river. They breed at Roswell Pits and also use the rivers for 
foraging.

This had been a great local jaunt, and although with relatively routine things on display, there

### Ely Common and Prickwillow Meadow at Roswell Pits

My previous visit to Ely Common this year on 5th July was pretty amazing. I remember the 
abundance of Essex skippers in particular that day. I was therefore keen to give it a late season 
scan to see how things had changed.

I headed over from the Black Fen Way entrance, down Prickwillow Road. However, I was conscious 
that I had originally considered doing a full loop on Black Fen Way that would have taken in some 
more meadows on the way past Ely sailing club. At this point, I therefore back-tracked to one of 
those meadows first before reaching the Common.

### <em>Prickwillow Meadow</em>

The view back over Roswell Pits is one of the best there is, and always deserves a picture.

The meadow behind the sailing club, borders Prickwillow Road and I had not been there for a long 
time. A path runs along the edge near the blackthorn hedge. It was dry but lucious. The grasses 
were long, and it was like hay in places. The blackthorn here though was incredible. It runs all 
the way along this meadow, and rivals even some of that at Bernwood Meadows we had seen on one of 
our other jaunts! It strongly reminded me of that. On the face if it, it is potentially very suited 
to brown and maybe black hairstreaks! Again, wishful thinking given they are not here, but the 
habitat is. Interesting. I still scanned it though, just, in, case (of a miracle).

I didn’t see it again.

I had a mooch around the Common a little more, following the blackthorn section rather that 
heading straight up the middle which had been mown since I was last here. In fact a large 
section of the meadow further down had been cut, in keeping with hay meadow management. Some parts 
had been left as well and here, brown argus, common blue and small heath were all present in

Today had been great. This was almost like three jaunts in one, with Black Fen Way, Prickwillow 
Meadow, and then Ely Common Wildspace. The highlight was obviously the clouded yellow, but the 
amount of great encounters and species escapades was quite frankly spectacular. You don’t always 
need a rarity to make a trip this much fun.
