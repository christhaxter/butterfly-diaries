## Adonis aplenty at Devil’s Dyke

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/Adonis_blue_cover.JPG"
       alt="Adonis blue">
</figure>

A slightly late decision to return to my favourite spot, Devil’s Dyke (DD) to search for 
butterflies. The memory of heatwaves and 30 degree sunny weather had long since faded, left now 
with repeated low pressure systems passing over. The rain was sorely needed though. This bank 
holiday weekend had seen a gap in the rain showers. Sunday was pleasant. However, Monday, the 
weather had turned windier, gusting up to 25 mph, with dark cumulus nearly sealing the sky at 
times, but, some sunshine was punching through. Temperatures were up to 18-20 degrees. Being a 
bank holiday in the UK, I decided to chance it at around 11 am.

On arrival, it was utter confusion. A sign greeted me that DD was actually…closed!

  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/uh_oh.jpg"

Ok, so that’s not good. I nearly headed back home there and then. However, on checking online, 
the previous day there was an event held where members of the public can ride their horses over 
the Newmarket racecourse that borders DD. It turns out that the public right of way was in fact 
open, and so I suspected that this sign had been accidentally left out. This was confirmed with 
no other entrances further down being blocked, and lots of other people using the walk. However, 
to be safe, I skipped the first embankment at first (hereafter E1, 2 etc to save words) and 
entered further down where there were no barriers.

I had seen barely any buterflies at this point, save a few small whites. Climbing to the top of 
the second embankment, it was visually spectacular. The images don’t quite convey it was mostly 
cloudy and very windy. But, glass half full, it was lovely to be out!

After spooking a green woodpecker, again, as I do on every visit here, I then immediately saw a 
flash of blue just at the top of E2…

Yes an Adonis blue!
  
OK that turned around pretty quick after nearly quitting the trip a few moments ago.

Just look at the blue in the top one, probably my best Adonis pic of the whole season. It was a 
bit more worn around the fringes and had silvering on the underside; perhaps the wear ‘n tear 
reflected the fact it had been a good two weeks since I was last here, giving enough time for 
some abrasion of the edges of wings. It was more than happy to show off its blue though 💙.

And then, just a bit further along….another one! They do like these carline thistles.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/Adonis_blue_3.JPG"
       alt="A tatty Adonis blue #2">
  <figcaption>A tatty Adonis blue #2</figcaption>

This one was very tattered. However, if you had to ask for a bit of wing removal so you can see 
the blue, and leave that indented back edge hind-wing spot still visible, as a diagnostic marker, 
you would have probably picked this exact wear pattern! This was amazing to see two in such quick 
succession though.

Small heaths were also appearing more and more as I walked.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/Small_heath.JPG"
       alt="Peekaboo">

I had also come across a familiar cutting out of the chalk. I headed a little bit lower down some 
grassy lower levels, and spotted what I first thought was a brightly coloured slug. It turns out 
this was a goat moth caterpillar! They are considered nationally scarce according to Butterfly 
Conservation, so this was a good find. I didn’t get close enough close enough to see if it smelt 
of goat though (as is a fact!).

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/Goat_moth.JPG"
       alt="Goat moth caterpillar">

One thing I always forget in these posts are the crickets. They are absolutely everywhere here and 
outnumber butterflies massively on the grassy slopes. They jump out and fly/glide away from their 
disturbed places to safer ones far away from my size 11s hitting the paths.

I was doing my usual trick of moving up and down the slopes on the small paths for FOMO. Back up 
to the top, a kestrel glided by – I wondered if it was the same male I had seen here last time, 
as it favoured a tree further along where I had flushed one last time. I had also noted that there 
were no chalk hill blues here yet today. That was quite a drop off from weeks gone by and the 
giddy heights of the days when they were dancing around my feet.

I had reached the end of the second embankment. Climbing up to the third, I passed a family 
and got talking to them. They were visiting from further north and were interested in what I 
had seen. I told them about the Adonis blues and in return they regailed me with a sighting of 
an emperor moth they had in their garden. Very cool indeed – I was not sure that I had ever seen 
one before. We parted ways and I pushed onto the third section.

Then, another Adonis!
  
<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/Adonis_blue_4.JPG"

Outstanding. This one was just basking. It was also beginning to match the other sightings I had 
read of higher numbers of Adonis – I had only really seen them in the tail of the 1st brood, 
recording singles in dramatic last ditch fashion, in what was clearly the start of the 2nd brood 
a couple of weeks back. At this point on the walk I had seen more Adonis than common blues.

That started to change though with a common blue then showing well towards the end of the third 
embankment.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/Common_blue_1.JPG"

I had not seen a brown argus yet, which played on my mind a bit. Perhaps I was being impatient as 
they were likely further down or keeping lower in the tall grasses. I passed another dog walker 
on the tops, whilst I peered down scanning the carline thisles and other nectar sources. A big 
dark cloud had also drifted over threatening rain, but stepping down out of the brisk wind 
blowing off the tops of E3, I came across the spot I mentioned in previous posts where the 
ground flattens and transitions to a chalk grassy section before E4. This location is where 
I had an Adonis blue some weeks back.

At first, it was quiet, without any sign of activity. Moving through this section, two male 
common blues then battled in flight, their more purple blue very likely not adonis. But then 
two butterflies locked in a mating position dropped onto a grass stem, waving in the wind, 
and the male had quite a bright blue I noticed.

And there they were…

A mating pair of Adonis!

The male here was a bit ‘common’, looking though, but the black lunules on the wing edges are 
there. The female is as clear as day. This was not a pair of chalk hill blues from the flash of 
blue of the male had shown, and also being too small. AI agreed. It was quite amazing to find 
them, and actually my first female Adonis too.

A quick scan around this area revealed no immediate signs of other Adonis blues, but there were 
common blues and finally a brown argus.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Devils_Dyke/2026_08_31/Common_blue_2.JPG"

The weather felt against me at this point so I chose not to go to E4 this time. Turning back, I 
reached E3 again to return climb, but first had spotted another blue cruise by. Tracking it to 
my right, I found it again.

Yep, it was another male Adonis!

Again the chequering and lunules were slight but they are a bit clearer in the second shot at 
the base of the hindwing. Clearly that blue was eeking to pop out too. I also saw another Adonis 
shortly after this but failed to photograph it making that seven so far. I also couldn’t help 
snapping a frame of one of the many grass veneer moths that join the crickets in launching from 
grass stem to stem.

At this point, a bird of prey caught my eye above me. I fumbled for camera settings but failed 
miserably at getting the shot properly. I switched to binoculars and wow, it was an osprey! 
This lined up well with their migration timing.

Arguing arguses has ring to it.

At the start of E3, before approaching E2, I also spotted lots of common blues and brown argus 
down by the side of the racecourse, again checking for any Adonis but none were here. However, 
one did show briefly again on E2, but again it flew on by after it had paused. It could have 
been one I already had seen but I counted it as #8.

On the home stretch though I pushed on over to E1, having skipped it at the start. I was 
confident I could find another Adonis there too. The sun had come out a bit more too, so this 
felt like a good opportunity.

However, after scouring the slopes, I had come up dry at E1 this time – no Adonis were here, 
well perhaps they were just hiding. I found another couple of brown argus and common blue nearer 
the start.

I had to work really hard for that little wonderful (not the word I used) common blue to check 
what it was. That picture is almost at the bottom of E1. I had also passed two other folks on 
the side of E1 that looked like they were also butterflying. After a quick nod was given, I 
decided at this point as it was quite windy still, it was time to call it a day and head back.

This trip had been somewhat unorthodox. It had been an uncertain start, nearly quitting before 
even beginning, and the conditions had been imperfect. But actually, if you had said, I would 
see eight Adonis and an osprey, wow, yes I would have taken it. Also, with the conditions today, 
I wouldn’t be suprised if there were quite a few more adonis blues around.

On the list of things not seen today, the most obvious one was chalk hill blue. Sadly any 
stragglers were just keeping a low profile or were really not there. Also, meadow brown. Not one! 
Interesting. No clouded yellow this time either but I can’t complain having seen them on the last 
few consecutive trips. Also no queen of Spain fritillary, as if 😀. All in all, I was "chalking" 
this one up as a success.
