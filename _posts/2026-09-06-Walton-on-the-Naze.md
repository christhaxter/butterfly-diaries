## Hopes for a royal flush: A jaunt to Walton-on-the-Naze

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/Wall_brown_1.JPG"
       alt="Wall brown">
</figure>

I wanted to squeeze in another coastal trip this season. If I am honest, this was not a random 
choice to visit Walton-on-the-Naze, it was targeted at migrant butterflies. A Facebook post noted 
a Queen of Spain Fritillary had been sighted here five days earlier (a tatty one at that). Again 
I was interested more in the idea of migrant butterfly arrival and the possibility of discovering 
something different in a close by coastal habitat. I was keeping this distinct from a twitch. So 
was this worth a punt? Yeah why not.

It was a sunny weekend. One thing in particular peaked my interest, the wind was turning southerly 
with a smidgen of easterly different from the Saturday, which was more westerly. In fact a Monarch 
had been sighted nearby in Suffolk on Saturday, from America, or an escapee? But a southerly could 
be interesting – could it push anything interesting over from the continent? Long-tailed blue? 
Maybe. This was always a needle in haystack search, and I was there more to enjoy the location, 
but you never know.

My partner joined me for this adventure, and after a short hop down to the coast we arrived at 
the site around 11 am. It was cloudy at first, but that melted away within the first half an hour. 
We headed towards the discovery centre. This was an amazing visitor centre to start the trip, 
packed full of info, with a neat shop, coffee, ice cream, lunch etc. The amenities around the 
site were very on point.

We passed through the discovery centre, and onto the paths. But first, a big portrait picture 
of the Naze.

Tall. My new 100-400 mm camera lens was also deployed poised to snap away. 😋 There are loads 
of information signs dotted around.

As on the sign above, the site is managed by the Essex Wildlife Trust and is a headland that 
covers lots of habitats such as the grassy flower-rich meadows, gorse, blackthorn and light 
woodlands above the cliffs, through to the beaches, shingle, saltmarsh and intertidal areas 
further around the coast at Hamford Water. The site is also a 50 million-year geological trip 
back into the past through the fossils that can be found under the cliffs.

Bird-wise and butterfly-wise, the site is a migrant hotspot. I forgot to photograph the recent 
bird sightings board in the visitor centre, but it included some interesting things such as wood 
warbler seen a few days before.

I couldn’t resist a visit to the cliff edge to start (by some safety rails). Immediately I could 
see that there was a lot of buddleia still in flower below the cliff. I figured that could be one 
hell of a draw for hungry insect arrivals. I scannerld, but sadly no rarities were here, however 
a hummingbird hawkmoth was one of the first things I noted, and painted lady and red admiral were 
there with a few small whites on display.

The views back out to sea were stunning, and I couldn’t resist a pic of the nearby Gunfleet Sands 
offshore wind farm.

We headed onwards along the cliff top and through the paths.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/View_2.jpg"

A few small birds were flitting around but were just so elusive I couldn’t relocate them. 
Goldfinches and linnets flew over. One other thing – the brambles here. Wow. There were no 
flowers of course being now nearly autumn, but the fruit here was so abundant, there were tonnes 
of ripe blackberries everywhere. The views back along the coast were also very impressive.

We had also coincided with a small tour that was taking place. However, back to butterflies, 
as the path went through a small bit of woodland – I think oak – a speckled wood dropped in.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/Speckled_wood.JPG"

Making our way on, the path came out into a grassy section, with lots of ragwort – so far there 
hadn’t been too much in the way of flowers around. Here, a small heath darted out on a thistle head.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/Small_heath.JPG"

This was a nice area. It then led out onto the top of the sea wall heading now out to the John 
Weston Nature Reserve, although we didn’t go that far, for some reason, and I wish we had 
looking back!

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/View_5.jpg"

A couple of times I was startled as orange butterflies passed by, but mainly each time it was a 
painted lady. Except this time. This time it was a wall brown! It posed actually quite well.

<figure class="report-photo-stack">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/Wall_brown_1.JPG"

This was great, as only the day before I had one in our garden in Ely. By contrast, here they 
were in their stronghold at the coast and I probably ended up with at least three individuals,
the one above being a female.

Making our way down, we diverted out to the sea direction and here another wall brown showed as 
well as a small white.

<figure class="report-figure">

We shuffled then out onto the sand and looped back around to where the path was. But here, birds 
were taking centre stage. A ringed plover called and showed well.

There were sanderling scuttling along the shore and a flock of black-headed gulls mixed with 
sandwich terns now all into winter plumage.

A little egret foraged further out. I should’ve said also our visit was all at mostly low tide. 
Two cormorants flew along the coast and various other waders, such as curlew, oystercatcher, 
redshank I heard. Views again, very pretty and Harwich was visible clearly in the distance.

We reached a sign giving information about the little terns that breed at Hamford.

Obviously it was too late for that. But as above, we didn’t make it that far. The tour had also 
caught us up and was heading further around. There were quite a few other folks out today, some 
with photo gear like me, others just out for a walk. I was feeling a bit tired to be honest here, 
and we instead looped back along the sea wall from where we came. We dropped down off the path 
into the grasses and along side bramble and blackthorn. I was curious too, maybe brown hairstreak 
were here? After scanning a few thistle heads, I didn’t see any, but I did wonder. Instead we were 
treated to lots of small heaths, and the first of the common blues of the day. A brown argus also 
showed.

Just prior to dropping down from the sea path, a painted lady had drifted in front of us and a 
few people were coming towards us. From the bottom, I saw two people then remark at something, 
which I assumed was the painted lady, but seconds later, a clouded yellow powered perpendicular 
across our path from the top! I figured it was probably that they had seen – no pic of course 
as again, as it was a butterfly on a mission.

We then moved onto a different path that diverted off away from the sea inland, but it was 
attractive as it has lots of ragwort lining the sides. However, the butterflies were fairly 
quiet here – common blue again though was present as were a couple of small whites. This path 
then joined up to where we had passed some way back, near to the speckled wood sighting in fact.

We then were headed back in the direction of the Naze. (You can actually climb up the Naze, but 
we didn’t this time.)

The place is steeped in history too, with lots of information around telling you about the 
importance of the site during wartime, as you can see below, from a sign I clearly took a 
picture of.

<figure class="report-figure">

We had actually done this route a bit too quickly, and I felt I wanted to scout out the site a 
bit more. Could something interesting be lurking that we missed? We therefore headed back for a 
quick bit of lunch before venturing back out again.

Again stopping by the buddleia area first, more painted ladies were here than before, brought 
out by the sunnier skies now.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/Painted_lady_2.JPG"

We stomped along a bit quicker this time as I felt that if anywhere a rarity was likely, it 
sheltered spots near the beach that we had come across. However, nothing showed. In fact I 
recall fewer butterflies on the second route out than the first – by now the sun was fully out 
and temperatures were up to around 25 degrees.

Back by the sea, however, we thought a nice alternative was to walk back along the beach. This 
was a good choice as it was picturesque.

Here, it was clear you could spend hours having fun sifting through fallen remnants of the cliff 
for fossils. We had no time to do that today but I am sure lots could be found. An outline of 
Homer Simpson had also been drawn in the sand – I bet you weren’t expecting that sentence ;).

Further down, we made our way up some small steps, and before we knew it, we were back beneath 
the Naze and the buddleia patch at the top of the cliff. This path is called crag walk.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/Crag_walk.jpg"

I decided it was worth a look at the base of the cliff section as here there was quite a bit 
of common fleabane.

I was searching all the blues, just in case one was a long-tailed (!). No, only common blues.

In the corner of that shot above when going through the images I noticed…. a long-winged 
conehead grasshopper lurking in the corner! That was a bonus, just looking at me as if to say, 
“what on earth are you doing?”

This area was nice. It was out of the wind a bit more here, and among the battling male common 
blues, a small copper added to the species tally.

Nice. It wasn’t possible to look at the buddleias at the cliff top from below being too far 
away. We made our way back up to the top up some steps, but the view here was again amazing.

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/Walton-on-the-Naze/View_8.jpg"

Back on the top, we were done. We had racked up over 10K steps and so needed refreshments, 
in the form of ice cream.

After that, and another quick check of the infamous buddleia patch (there are probably others 
but this looked the best), we made our way back through the discovery centre, being about 15:30 
by now and left it there.

As an epilogue to this trip, back home, I found a small tortoiseshell on our buddleia – yes it 
has been weekend of buddleia mentions in my posts. I always delight in seeing them, which isn’t 
too often these days, now far fewer in number than years gone by.

There had been no rarities today, but it had a good stroll around a very pretty coastal spot. 
If we come back, we will head further around. At other times of the year, it would be great for 
large skipper, probably green hairstreak, and the other birding spots are a must see. This had 
been a good site to find and try out, and I was happy with the clouded yellow and wall brown we 
had seen, making up a pretty impressive species tally for this stage in the season.
