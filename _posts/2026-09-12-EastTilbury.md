## A Queen’s gambit: nobody expects the Spanish Inquisition in Essex

<figure class="report-figure">
  <img src="/butterfly-diaries/assets/images/QOSF.JPG"
       alt="Queen of Spain firtillary">
</figure>

OK, so I couldn’t resist it. There had been reports of an influx of Queen of Spain fritillary 
down in Essex at East Tilbury. This a rare migrant species, that is occasionally blown over to UK shores from 
the near continent. This year had so far turned out to be rather exceptional for sightings across 
southerly UK coasts.

Handling of this blog however needed to be done sensitively originally. The site became 
ecologically very important in a short space of time. It has since been reported in the Guardian, 
but I am still just erring on the side of caution and leaving you to put in some legwork 
to find out where the site was. I am also redacting the precise details and pictures I normally post¤. 
Nothing mean-spirited here, just precaution. Anyway, back to it.

My previous excursion over to Walton-on-the-Naze the weekend before had been because I suspected 
there could be another influx of Queen of Spain fritillary (QOSF) ariving on a southerly breeze, 
or an eruption of local populations from an earlier arrival. This actually had come to pass with 
these latest sightings, which kind of vindicated that previous trip (which came up dry). A large 
number sightings had been reported down in the southwest around Cornwall and Dorset and also in 
Essex, the latter geographically closer to me, so was worth a look. I had obviously not become 
obsessed with this species in any way 🙃.

Another deciding factor regarding to go for this particular trip was that it was possible by rail. 
It was a couple of hours driving time, which was tempting, but as this was a twitch, I felt that 
I had to be green on this one. Plus, I wasn’t fully sure just how many people knew about this 
site, and whether there would be more pressure on the area because of folks like me. So the 
train felt like a good idea.

The weather too – wow it had been scheduled to be wall-to-wall sunshine. Temperatures too were 
forecast to be good, 18 to 24 degrees, wind ok, a bit breezy maybe. It was suspiciously perfect. 

That suspicion was well founded though when the day came around, as the weather was much cloudier 
than predicted, particularly over London and the south east. Then, the trains were disrupted. On 
arrival at Ely station, I saw that the 07:19 I had planned to catch to Kings Cross was cancelled. 
There was a shortage of train drivers, which had put pay to many regular services. I asked a train 
guard what my options were to get to London. I had to get the train to Liverpool Street instead, 
with an additional change at Cambridge. This actually only delayed me by 30 mins in the end.

Once in London, given it was early, there was a calm and just-getting-going kind of vibe. I was 
quite on edge for this trip though. As a self confessed anxious traveller, I find it hard to 
chill out, and the excitement of finding QOSF was fanning the flames. Not that you need to know 
any of this, as you’re probably thinking, get to the point and show me some butterflies, but 
the grind is part of it.

On arrival at the local station near the site, the pieces had fallen into place. I had done the 
homework, but I was starting to feel like the probability of seeing a QOSF had dropped with the 
cloud cover. I could see the edge of the cloud bank in the distance on the train as I was pulling 
into the station. It was predicted to break, so I needed some patience and belief that it would 
improve.

I made my way through a local park to get to the site¤.

The path then takes you to an agricultural setting, with crops that seemed to have been left by 
the farmer, and therefore contained a wide variety of flowers mixed in; oxtongue I think, and 
thistles – my botany game was not strong today.

Along this first stretch of path, as it arced around the edge of the field,  some observations 
had been made for QOSF. Having now unpacked my Nikon camera rig, I started scanning around. It 
was 10:50 by now. I was definitely prepared for this to be less straightforward than I had 
expected. However, there were some postive signs. Some small whites had taken to the wing.

Temperatures were clearly good enough for warmed up butterfly flight muscles, but the Queen of 
Spain fritillary is a sun lover, and needs a bit more of a kick start to bring them out from 
their perching and sheltering spots.

At this first part of the track, a fellow enthusiast was on the same path walking with purpose 
behind me. We exchanged greetings, asking if I was here for the Queens, which is a sentence 
needing some qualification, yes the butterflies!
  
I was taking my careful approach, soaking it all in. However, not much else was showing. I 
reached the other side of the field where oxtongue and some thistles, plus geranium and other 
species were present in abundance – an amazing nectar source. The other fella had carried on 
further ahead towards a second field, but as I approached this nectar banquet, another chap was 
also searching further into the field. At this point, we were a team of three all out with one 
thing on our minds. But where were the QOSF hiding? We were searching bare ground patches, stems, 
nectar just covering all bases. 

We continued in a slightly separated fashion moving further around the path of the second field, 
but just prior to this, a flash of browny orange – it was a wall. I had not got my eye in yet for 
colours of the QOSF. I found it hiding on the ground in amongst the (I want to say wheat) crop.

Walls were actually very abundant. I estimated probably ten or so on the day.

The section we were in now in the second field was the supposed hotspot, but there were still no 
signs of QOSF. All it needed was that little bit of sun injection to get them basking and nectaring. 
Here we were on a fairly good field path next to the crop, bordering some trees. Just a bit 
further around, the ground transitioned to some more paved concrete disused areas before it 
went towards some houses. Although more man-made, this end part also felt plausible with various 
nectar and basking spots available. This was about the end of the extent of the main ‘site’ but 
we had so far only covered the edge of one side of the fields.

One of our party detoured off on a rather makeshift track actually into the field through the 
crops – it was fine to do this apparently as the farmer had let it go – that was a massive 
advantage of having local people here knowing this!
  
He was looking back my way repeatedly from across the other side. He then raised his hand.

YES.

He had found one.

I moved briskly over along the trampled wheat path and after a few seconds, the flash of light 
orange whizzed past us and over to a bank or ditch of fruited brambles and other goodness. I had 
ended up in front of him at this point, closer to the bank trying to track it, but lost it part 
way along. We were essentially on the opposite side of the field here from the urban end point. 
It must have dived down off into the ditch. This was technically a sighting so, yeah it counts! 
That helped ease my tension slightly, but I wanted more…


•


At this point, a fourth chap had made his way to us, and we discussed weather radars and 
predictions on our phones. We were now four strong.

The breeze was a little high at this point and a different direction to previous days making 
prior hotspots of sightings less predictable. After some deliberation, we felt that a good spot 
would be around the back edge of the field where the trees today were creating a more sheltered 
and warmer spot. So we edged around the border of the field to investigate.

The vegetation was high here, and on the side that I was on, it was thick brash in places with 
nettles – shorts felt like a bad choice at this point, and ticks were on my mind, but these are 
the hazards of the field! Around the back edge of the field it was indeed calmer, with lots of 
high growing oxtongue and some thisles. I was doing the usual search tactics and found a tattered 
brown argus.

with the low aperture melting the background beautifully.

We were finding common darter dragonflies quite a bit. I think I saw an emperor dragonfly a 
bit later on too.

We made our way continuing anticlockwise. The edge of the field runs all the way back to the 
entrance to this second field, and so we headed that way, more or less back to where we had 
the fleeting QOSF sighting. I then diverted away back to the main path, and we were all 
slightly scattered observing different field sides. I found another common blue.

Back nearer the field #2 entrance, someone then said “got one!”. It could’ve been the QOSF 
we flushed before, but equally could have been another. We all followed this one as it fluttered 
over the corn crop. But somehow, it had slipped us by anyway! Drat. At least I had the size and 
lighter orange colour in mind now.

I can’t quite remember how this next part happened, but we relocated either same QOSF we had all 
just lost, or found another but we followed it in flight. I actually snapped it badly mid flight, 
but it was tracked to a tall growing lambs quarters (the plant!) stem where it then landed. And 
there, wowzers – YES!!!

butterfly moments of the season. I snapped a bunch of pictures, taking advantage of my new 400 
mm lens plus crop sensor reach playing with shutter, aperture and AF-C focus. I took a short 
video just before it took wing.

Well, there we go. That was species 49 for me! We had a collective ‘yeah’ moment. We then 
spotted a clouded yellow fly by, while I was crouched down trying to get those underwing QOSF 
shots.

I was more relaxed now. But given I was physically battling against the aftermath of earlier 
anxieties, I was perhaps keen to leave it there. However, this is where it gets really 
interesting as it wasn’t the end of the story.

As I made my way back along the fringe of field 1, I was thinking to myself how much I would’ve 
loved to have ‘my’ QOSF sighting, i.e. not one someone else found. That sounds a bit selfish 
but there is a satisfaction of finding something yourself and I know first-hand how high that 
dopamine hit is.

coming towards me very kindly and deliberately slowed her pace knowing I was busy photographing 
the path (from her view anyway). The sun was making it feel really quite warm now, around 13:10 
by this point. I moved further around the field edge, back towards the point where I had entered

packing my gear away, I paused, had a bag of crisps and reassessed the time. I had about 30 
minutes before the train. What was the point of just sitting on the platform when I could be 
enjoying the QOSF more? A mad choice maybe, but I backtracked and went back out to the field 
again. I am glad I did.

This one was just dancing around me. That may be a contender too for the shot of the day, with 
the 45 degree angle showing both under and upper wings. I had to keep moving around to get 
behind it, as it just wanted to bask facing the sun. It is distinct from the others from the 
small worn black spot at the bottom right of the right forewing. And this one also then fought 
with another tussling in mid air! That ‘other’ QOSF, I am calling #5, likely dealing males here. 
I mean two at the same time! Incredible.

I had to think about making my way back (again). It was 13:35, 15 mins until the train. Moving 
now back around towards the first field entrance point, it was only a minute later, I then saw

Just look at the eye! This one was identifiable by a slightly tatty right forewing. This was 
truly amazing, with two sets of territorial males, and the fact I was now logging individuals 
by wing damage was just out of this world.

OK, I had really pushed it for time though. I could easily have spent all afternoon here, and 
part of me wishes I had. It was an honor to spend time with them. However with only ten minutes 
to the train, I really had to leg-it.

I made the train, but had to deal with repercussions of being anxious earlier on. But when I 
got back, reminiscing, it was absolutely 💯 worth the trip, and to have done it all on the 
rails, felt really good.

The final tally of QOSF for me after I left the group was six! I mean wow, that was ridiculous 
given I was about to leave after the first underside perched one, which was not even part of 
the six. In total, add two more to mine from the group, at least. I think one of the fellas 
had well into double figures and I expect a final tally nearer 20 perhaps could be possible. 
I didn’t see the others on my second reccy, but who knows. It certainly tallied with the 
numbers others had recorded days before. Apparently they also had females here. That makes 
it distinct from the other obvious comparison site down in Hampshire this year, that had only 
males as I understood. The QOSF do like these arable field margins and you only find if you 
go and look. I regret not having more time to explore the wider area, but maybe we will return 
next year.

What a trip this had been, plus the clouded yellow bonus 💛. And to have seen the duke 🩶, the 
emperor 💜 and the queen 🧡 all in the same season, was royally satisfying.
