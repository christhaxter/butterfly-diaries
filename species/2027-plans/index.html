Plans for 2027

As the 2026 season closed, I had reached 49 UK butterfly species. That felt like a good jump in a single year. I had seen some amazing species, including purple emperor, swallowtail and black hairstreak. Of course, I will revisit them all. For now, though, attention turns to whether I can add more species to the total and break into the 50s during 2027.

The challenge is becoming a multi-year quest. There are still some particularly exciting species to find, and some will require dedicated trips to specific habitats at just the right time of year. I have sketched out a possible itinerary, although weather, butterfly numbers and the practicalities of travelling will inevitably have the final say.

Early spring: a very early start

I started late in 2026, so I intend to start super early in 2027. In fact, one somewhat mad idea would be to target a rare species that may be increasing: the large tortoiseshell.

In late March or early April, weather permitting, we could make a trip to Knepp in Sussex, hoping to encounter any adults that have overwintered and emerged. This would need to be an overnight trip, and there is a considerable chance of seeing nothing at all. It would be an ambitious start to the season, but potentially a very exciting one.

Green hairstreak

One of the species I missed as a target in 2026 is already on my life list, having seen it in my youth. However, I still need a decent photograph of the green hairstreak.

There are several possibilities. Kelling Heath in Norfolk is one option, while Devil's Dyke would be closer to home. Closer still, apparently only about five minutes away, is Chettisham Meadow, a Wildlife Trust site. It would be rather satisfying to get a better photograph of this species almost on my doorstep.

May: fritillaries and skippers

Pearl-bordered fritillary

The pearl-bordered fritillary is missing from my list and would be a great addition. Its flight period runs from early May into mid to late June, although mid to late May may be the best time to try.

We would need to travel further afield, with Sussex offering some possibilities. Rewell Wood, near Arundel in West Sussex, could be a good option. We like Arundel, having visited before, so perhaps this could be combined with a weekend away and a bit of a holiday.

Chequered skipper

The chequered skipper is another species I need to catch up with. The natural colonies are a long way away, in Argyll and Bute around Oban, somewhere we have also visited previously.

However, there may be a cheat code: a reintroduced population derived from Belgium, much closer to home at Fineshade Wood, managed by Forestry England. It is about an hour and fifteen minutes away from us. I hope it counts as a legitimate sighting, because it would save a much longer journey!

I had been thinking of late May or early June, although the Forestry England information identifies June as the best time. I will need to check the latest guidance closer to the season.

Marsh fritillary

This is probably the species I am most looking forward to finding. The marsh fritillary was on my 2026 list, and it remains a particularly attractive butterfly that I would love to see.

I tried my luck at a potentially introduced population at Market Weston Fen in Suffolk, but for 2027 I intend to try Chambers Farm Wood in Lincolnshire. It has a good population and seems to be the nearest go-to location for a more concerted attempt, at around two hours away.

The flight period is generally late May to early July, so early June looks like a sensible target, assuming the season is early again.

June: the blues and more specialist species

Large blue

We had previously planned to try for the large blue at Rodborough Common, but had to change our plans at the last minute. This is still a species I really want to see in 2027.

We could try Rodborough again, or head further afield to Collard Hill in Somerset, which may offer a better chance. That could be achievable from Tewkesbury, around an hour and a half away, if we are already visiting relatives in the area. It would be a trip within a trip, probably around mid to late June.

Timing will be critical, as the flight window is relatively short. These trips will need to be treated as holidays, so that my green guilt does not consume me!

Being around Gloucestershire would also give us a possible backup for marsh fritillary. If Chambers Farm Wood does not work out, Strawberry Banks, a Gloucestershire Wildlife Trust nature reserve, could be another option.

Early July: a northern triple-header?

Moving into summer, I need to pivot north. There are several species I still have not seen: high brown fritillary, small pearl-bordered fritillary and mountain ringlet. In an ideal world, we could combine all three in one northern trip in early July.

High brown fritillary

The high brown fritillary flies from mid-June to late July. We had this in mind in 2026 when visiting the northern stronghold around Arnside in northern England, but narrowly missed the flight window.

There are several promising sites to consider, including Gait Barrows and Arnside Knott. It would be good to get the timing right this time.

Mountain ringlet

The mountain ringlet also flies from mid-June to late July, so it could potentially fit into the same northern trip. Haweswater or Irton Fell are possibilities, with the aim of finding a site at a lower altitude that is accessible without a massive hike.

This is one where the practicalities matter. I would love to see the species, but the plan needs to be achievable as well as exciting.

Small pearl-bordered fritillary

The small pearl-bordered fritillary may be the awkward one to fit in. Early July could be past peak or towards the end of the flight period at northern sites, but there may still be opportunities in suitable damp habitats, including coastal dunes, wet mosses and valley mires.

Possible sites to investigate include Drigg Dunes National Nature Reserve near Ravenglass, Barkbooth Lot, Finglandrigg Woods National Nature Reserve and Smardale Hill. These are options to research further rather than a final itinerary.

I would also like a better photograph of the large heath. I saw one briefly in 2026, and a northern trip could give me another opportunity to spend more time with this species.

Late July: another southern trip?

There may be a gap in proceedings towards the end of July. We could revisit local sites for some of the interesting butterflies already seen during 2026, or make another trip further afield.

Scotch argus

At Arnside Knott in Cumbria, late July can offer the chance of seeing the scotch argus. Unfortunately, that would mean another trip north, so it may be one to leave for a future season if the early-July trip already involves a lot of travelling.

Lulworth skipper

An alternative would be a southern trip to Dorset for the Lulworth skipper. Bindon Hill, overlooking Lulworth Cove, could be worth exploring, particularly where tor-grass grows. It would, however, be another long excursion requiring an overnight stay.

August: second chances and late-season surprises

If I miss the small pearl-bordered fritillary earlier in the summer, there may be a chance to target a partial second-brood emergence further south in August.

I will also keep an eye out for late-summer rarities, such as the long-tailed blue, or even hold out hope for something really special like a Camberwell beauty. These would be opportunistic encounters rather than species I can sensibly build a trip around.

Further ahead: species still on the horizon

Glanville fritillary

One glaring omission from my plans is the Glanville fritillary. Finding this species would require a dedicated trip to the Isle of Wight, probably between mid-May and late June. I am not sure we can fit that into 2027, so it may be one to save for 2028.

Cryptic wood white

The cryptic wood white would require a trip to Northern Ireland, where the relevant sites would need to be visited in June. It is another good reason to keep this as a multi-year quest: there is always another part of the country, another habitat and another species to explore.

How much can I achieve?

If I manage half of these targets in 2027, I will be happy. Some species will inevitably prove elusive, and plans may change as the season unfolds. The fun will then become a matter of refinement: revisiting places, improving photographs and enjoying better encounters with species I have already seen.

I am not even thinking about the species just across the Channel in northern France. Not even slightly. All right, perhaps a little.

Beyond Britain

My European butterfly list is still very much a work in progress. The Valencia trip in October 2026 gave me the chance to explore Mediterranean habitats and add species including African grass blue and Lang's short-tailed blue. I also saw species such as southern small white and eastern bath white.

European trips open up a different set of possibilities, and I am keen to explore more of them in future. For now, though, the main challenge remains the same: keep exploring, keep learning about butterfly habitats, and see how far the quest can go.
