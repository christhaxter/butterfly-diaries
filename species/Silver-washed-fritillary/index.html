Silver-washed fritillary

Argynnis paphia

About the species

The Silver-washed Fritillary is a large, powerful butterfly associated with woodland. It is particularly characteristic of sunny rides and clearings, where adults fly rapidly between patches of light and shade. Its orange wings and energetic flight make it one of the more noticeable butterflies of midsummer woodland.

Despite its woodland habitat, it is often seen feeding in sunny openings, especially where Bramble and other flowers provide a plentiful source of nectar. Adults may also wander beyond woodland and occasionally turn up in gardens.

Identification

The Silver-washed Fritillary is a large butterfly with a wingspan of around 65–75 mm. The upperwings are bright orange with black spots and markings, while the underside of the hindwings has distinctive pale greenish-silver bands.

Males have four prominent black scent-marked stripes on the forewings, which help distinguish them from females. Females are generally larger and may be darker, with some individuals showing a greenish or brownish appearance.

The underside is particularly useful for identification. The hindwings have broad, pale bands that give the species its name, contrasting with the darker green and brown background.

Its large size and strong, gliding flight help distinguish it from smaller fritillaries. It often flies high along woodland rides before dropping down to feed or bask.

Habitat and distribution

Silver-washed Fritillaries are mainly found in deciduous woodland, particularly where sunny rides, glades and clearings provide a mixture of open space and shelter. Mature woodland with flowering Bramble and other nectar sources is especially attractive to adults.

The caterpillars feed on violets, which grow in the woodland understorey. Females lay their eggs on tree trunks or nearby surfaces close to suitable foodplants, and the newly hatched caterpillars overwinter before feeding the following spring.

Adults are usually seen from early summer into late summer. They may be encountered flying along woodland rides, feeding on Bramble blossom or visiting other flowers in sunny clearings.

The species occurs across parts of southern and central England and Wales, with more localised populations elsewhere. Maintaining suitable woodland structure, sunny rides and areas of violets helps support its populations.
