Black hairstreak

Satyrium pruni

About the species

The black hairstreak is one of Britain's rarest and most elusive butterflies, with a highly restricted distribution in central and southern England. It is closely associated with mature blackthorn scrub in woodland and hedgerow habitats, where adults spend much of their time high in the canopy or hidden among dense vegetation.

Adults are usually on the wing from early June to mid-July, although the flight period can vary between years. The season is particularly brief, often lasting only two or three weeks at individual sites. Despite their scarcity, adults can sometimes be seen flying in short, looping flights around the tops of trees, particularly during warm, sunny weather.

Identification

The black hairstreak is a small butterfly with a wingspan of approximately 34–40 mm. Both sexes have dark brown upperwings, with orange markings towards the outer edges. The upperwings are rarely seen clearly, as adults usually settle with their wings closed.

The underside is brown, with a distinctive orange-red band along the hindwing margins, bordered by a row of black spots with pale edges. A fine white line runs across the underside, although it is generally less pronounced than the characteristic W-shaped marking of the white-letter hairstreak.

The black hairstreak can be particularly difficult to distinguish from the white-letter hairstreak, especially in flight. The pattern of black spots along the orange hindwing band is an important identification feature, helping to separate the two species when viewed at close quarters.

Habitat and distribution

Black hairstreaks are strongly associated with mature, dense stands of blackthorn in sheltered woodland situations. They are particularly characteristic of sunny woodland edges, rides, glades and adjoining hedgerows, where blackthorn scrub provides suitable conditions for breeding.

The caterpillars feed primarily on blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), although wild plum (Prunus domestica) and other related species may occasionally be used. Females lay eggs individually on the twigs, often in sheltered positions around buds and small branches.

The eggs remain on the twigs through the winter, with the caterpillars hatching the following spring as the blackthorn buds begin to develop. The young larvae feed on the emerging leaves and shoots, eventually pupating among the surrounding vegetation.

Adults spend much of their time in the canopy of trees or within dense scrub, feeding mainly on honeydew produced by aphids. They may occasionally descend to feed on flowers such as wild privet, dog-rose and bramble, providing some opportunities to see them closer to ground level.

In Britain, the black hairstreak has a very restricted distribution, largely confined to a belt of habitat between Oxford and Peterborough, with its principal populations in Oxfordshire, Buckinghamshire and neighbouring parts of the East Midlands. Its limited ability to disperse means that colonies are particularly dependent on the continuity of suitable woodland and scrub habitats.
