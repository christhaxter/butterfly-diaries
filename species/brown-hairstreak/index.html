Brown hairstreak

Thecla betulae

About the species

The brown hairstreak is the largest of Britain's hairstreak butterflies, but as with the black hairstreak, it is also one of the most elusive. Adults spend much of their time high in the tree canopy, where they feed on honeydew produced by aphids. They are often associated with blackthorn and prominent ash trees along woodland edges and hedgerows, which act as gathering points for adults.

The species is single-brooded, with adults generally appearing from late July into September, sometimes extending into October. Despite their preference for the tree canopy, females can be seen lower down as they search for suitable places to lay their eggs.

Identification

The brown hairstreak has dark brown upperwings, with small orange tails on the hindwings. Females are particularly distinctive, with large, bright orange patches on the forewings; these markings are much less developed in males.

With the wings closed, the underside is a warm orange-brown, crossed by two fine, wavy white lines. The small tails and orange markings around the hindwing edge help complete its distinctive appearance. With a wingspan of around 38–40 mm, it is the largest of the British hairstreaks.

Habitat and distribution

Brown hairstreaks are found in scattered areas of southern and central England and south-west Wales. They favour landscapes with extensive networks of hedgerows, scrub and woodland edges, particularly where blackthorn is abundant.

The main larval foodplant is blackthorn (Prunus spinosa), with other related plum species occasionally used. Females lay their eggs singly on young blackthorn growth, often in sheltered positions. The eggs remain on the branches through the winter, hatching the following spring as the buds begin to open.

The caterpillars feed on the developing leaves and are well camouflaged against the foliage. They eventually descend to the ground to pupate amongst leaf litter and vegetation.

The species has declined in many parts of Britain, largely because of hedgerow removal and intensive hedge cutting. Eggs are particularly vulnerable to winter flailing, making sympathetic management of blackthorn hedges important for its conservation.
