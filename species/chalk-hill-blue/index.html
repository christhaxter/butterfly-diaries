Chalk hill blue

Polyommatus coridon

About the species

The chalk hill blue is one of Britain's largest blue butterflies, found mainly on chalk and limestone grasslands in southern England. It is a characteristic species of these flower-rich downland habitats, where it can sometimes occur in impressive numbers during the summer.

Adults are generally on the wing from July to September, with numbers often peaking during July and August. Males are particularly conspicuous, flying low over the grassland in search of females.

Identification

The chalk hill blue is a relatively large blue butterfly, with a wingspan of approximately 33–40 mm. Males have distinctive pale, silvery-blue upperwings with a dark brown border and a fine white fringe. In bright sunshine, their wings can appear almost milky blue.

Females are predominantly brown above, sometimes with a variable blue dusting near the body. They have orange markings along the edges of the hindwings and a white, chequered fringe. Their understated colouring can make them surprisingly easy to overlook among the grassland vegetation.

The underside of both sexes is greyish-brown, with prominent black spots, white markings and orange lunules along the hindwing edges. The combination of their relatively large size, pale underside and distinctive markings helps separate them from other blue butterflies. However, care is needed with identification, of females in particular, that can be confused with Adonis blue given both species shave a chequered fringe to the outer edges of wings.

Habitat and distribution

Chalk hill blues are closely associated with species-rich chalk and limestone grassland, particularly on sheltered, sunny slopes. These habitats support their essential larval foodplant, horseshoe vetch (Hippocrepis comosa), which is restricted to calcareous grassland.

Adults feed on a variety of flowers, including knapweeds, scabiouses and other grassland plants. Males can also be attracted to damp ground, animal dung and other sources of moisture and minerals.

The species is restricted to southern England in Britain, with its distribution closely linked to the availability of suitable chalk and limestone grassland. It has declined in distribution in recent decades, largely reflecting the loss and deterioration of these specialised habitats.

The caterpillars feed on Horseshoe Vetch and have an interesting relationship with ants, which attend them in return for sugary secretions. Eggs are laid singly on the foodplant or nearby vegetation during the summer and remain through the winter, with caterpillars emerging the following spring.
