Clouded yellow

Colias croceus

About the species

The clouded yellow is one of Britain's most striking migrant butterflies, arriving from southern Europe and North Africa. Its numbers vary considerably from year to year, depending on weather conditions and the strength of immigration. Most years bring relatively modest numbers, but occasionally large influxes produce what are known as 'clouded yellow years'.

It is a strong, fast-flying butterfly, often seen racing across open grassland in search of nectar and suitable breeding habitat. Although it can turn up almost anywhere, it is particularly associated with open, flower-rich landscapes, including chalk grassland and clover fields.

Clouded yellows can arrive in Britain from spring onwards, with further immigration possible through the summer. Early arrivals may breed here, producing subsequent generations that can be seen later in the season, sometimes well into October or November. However, the species rarely survives the British winter, so its presence remains largely dependent on fresh arrivals from the continent.

Identification

The clouded yellow is a medium-sized butterfly with striking golden-yellow to orange upperwings, edged with broad, dark borders. The forewings have a prominent black spot, while the hindwings have a distinctive dark spot containing an orange centre.

Males have largely solid black borders to the upperwings, whereas females have a series of yellow spots within the dark margins. Some females occur in a pale, creamy-white form known as helice, which can look quite different from the usual golden-yellow appearance.

When settled, clouded yellows usually keep their wings closed. The underside is a yellowish-green, with two silvery-white spots on the hindwing, often surrounded by a darker marking. Their rapid, purposeful flight and tendency to settle only briefly can make them surprisingly challenging to photograph.

Habitat and distribution

As a migrant, the clouded yellow can appear almost anywhere in Britain, although sightings are generally more frequent in southern England. It favours open habitats with plenty of nectar and suitable larval foodplants, particularly unimproved grassland, chalk downland, coastal grassland and clover fields.

The caterpillars feed on plants in the pea family, particularly clovers (Trifolium species), lucerne (Medicago sativa) and common bird's-foot trefoil (Lotus corniculatus). Females lay their eggs singly on the leaves of suitable foodplants, and the caterpillars can develop relatively quickly in favourable conditions.

Although the species can breed successfully in Britain, its ability to persist through the winter is very limited. Most populations depend on immigration from warmer parts of Europe and North Africa, with the numbers arriving each year strongly influenced by weather and conditions along their migration routes.
