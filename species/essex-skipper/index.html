Essex skipper

Thymelicus lineola

About the species

The Essex skipper is a small, fast-flying butterfly found mainly across southern England and Wales. It is closely related to the small skipper and can be extremely difficult to distinguish from it in the field. Despite its modest appearance, it has an interesting history, having expanded considerably from its original strongholds in south-east England over recent decades.

Adults are generally on the wing from late June to August, with numbers often peaking during July. They have a rapid, darting flight, usually keeping close to the grassland vegetation. When settled, they frequently bask on grass stems or visit flowers for nectar, making sunny summer days a good time to look for them.

Identification

The Essex skipper is a small butterfly with a wingspan of approximately 26–30 mm. Both sexes have predominantly orange-brown upperwings with darker brown borders and relatively plain markings. Males have a short, narrow dark scent brand running almost parallel to the leading edge of the forewing.

The species is remarkably similar to the small skipper, and separating the two reliably can require a close look. The most useful distinguishing feature is the underside of the antenna tips, which are distinctly black in the Essex skipper, compared with the orange-brown tips of the small skipper.

The male's scent brand can also help with identification. In the Essex skipper it is relatively short and straight, running parallel to the forewing edge, whereas in the small skipper it is generally longer and more curved. However, the antenna tips remain the most reliable identification feature.

Habitat and distribution

Essex skippers are found in a variety of grassy habitats, including rough grassland, roadside verges, woodland rides, field margins and sunny clearings. They are particularly associated with areas of taller, relatively dry grassland where suitable larval foodplants are abundant.

The main larval foodplant is cock's-foot (Dactylis glomerata), although several other grasses can also be used, including creeping soft-grass, common couch, timothy, meadow foxtail and tor-grass. Females lay their eggs within the sheaths of grasses, where they remain through the winter.

The caterpillars emerge the following spring and feed on the grasses, often sheltering within tubes of leaves spun together with silk. When fully grown, they pupate low down among the vegetation before emerging as adults during the summer.

The Essex skipper has expanded its British range considerably since it was first recognised as a separate species in the late nineteenth century. It is now widespread across much of southern England and has also become established in Wales, with scattered populations further north.
