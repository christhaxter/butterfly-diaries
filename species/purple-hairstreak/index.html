Purple hairstreak

Favonius quercus

About the species

The purple hairstreak is a small butterfly that spends much of its life high in the canopy of oak trees. It is widespread in parts of southern and central England, but can be surprisingly difficult to see, as it often flies above eye level.

On warm summer days, adults can sometimes be seen fluttering around oak crowns, particularly in the afternoon and early evening. Their distinctive purple colouring is often only visible when the wings catch the sunlight at just the right angle.

Identification

The purple hairstreak is a small butterfly with a wingspan of around 30–38 mm. The underside is grey, with a fine white streak running across both wings and small orange markings near the tail of the hindwings.

Males have a purple sheen across much of the upperwing, which can appear brilliant when viewed in direct sunlight. Females are generally brown above, with purple patches on the forewings.

The butterfly has small tails on its hindwings, accompanied by orange markings and dark eyespots on the underside. These features help distinguish it from other hairstreaks, although its small size and habit of flying high in trees can make identification difficult.

Habitat and distribution

Purple hairstreaks are closely associated with oak trees, including both native species, Pedunculate Oak and Sessile Oak. They occur in oak woodland, parkland, wooded landscapes and areas where mature oak trees are present.

Adults spend much of their time in the canopy, where they feed mainly on honeydew produced by aphids. Most sightings are of butterflies moving around the tops of trees. However, they can also descend to lower levels to feed on flowers or to bask, and occasionally even to the ground, where they may take up minerals.

The caterpillars feed on oak buds and flowers in spring. Eggs are laid individually near the buds during late summer, where they remain through the winter before hatching the following spring.

The species is widespread across much of southern and central England and Wales, becoming more localised further north. Its canopy-dwelling behaviour means it is often under-recorded, and watching oak trees on warm summer evenings can be a rewarding way to find it.
