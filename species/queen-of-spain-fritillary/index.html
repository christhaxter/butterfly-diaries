Queen of Spain fritillary

Issoria lathonia

About the species

The queen of Spain fritillary is a striking butterfly and a scarce migrant to Britain, arriving from continental Europe in variable numbers. Although widespread and often common across much of its European range, it remains an exciting and relatively unusual species to encounter in the UK.

It is a powerful flyer, capable of travelling considerable distances, and can turn up in a variety of open habitats, particularly during periods of favourable weather for migration. Sightings in Britain are often associated with coastal areas, although individuals can appear well inland.

In mainland Europe, the species can produce several generations during the year, with adults flying over much of the spring, summer and autumn. Its presence in Britain is largely dependent on immigration, although occasional breeding has been recorded.

Identification

The queen of Spain fritillary has orange-brown upperwings covered with numerous black spots and markings. The wings are relatively angular, particularly the hindwings, giving it a distinctive shape compared with many other fritillaries.

The most unmistakable feature is the underside of the hindwings, which is decorated with large, brilliant silver-white spots or 'mirrors'. These stand out against the orange-brown background and give the butterfly its characteristic appearance when settled with its wings closed.

It is a medium-sized fritillary, with a wingspan typically around 40–50 mm, although individuals can vary in size. The upperwing pattern is quite variable, with the black spots differing in size and prominence between individuals.

In flight, it can appear fast and erratic, sometimes resembling a small fritillary or even a wall brown at a distance. When settled, the distinctive silvery markings on the underside of the hindwings make identification straightforward.

Habitat and distribution

Across Europe, queen of Spain fritillaries occupy a variety of open, sunny habitats, including dry grassland, heathland, dunes, fallow ground and agricultural landscapes. They favour warm conditions and can often be found nectaring on thistles and other flowering plants.

The caterpillars feed primarily on violets, including wild pansy and field pansy. Eggs are laid singly on or near the foodplants, and the larvae develop relatively quickly in favourable conditions. The species can have several generations in warmer parts of its range, with the number depending on climate and location.

In Britain, the queen of Spain fritillary is a rare migrant, with sightings generally concentrated in southern and eastern England, particularly along the coast. Individuals may arrive from mainland Europe during suitable weather conditions, and occasionally remain long enough for breeding to take place.

Adults can potentially be encountered from late spring through to autumn, depending on immigration and local conditions. Its appearance in Britain is unpredictable, making sightings all the more exciting for butterfly enthusiasts.
