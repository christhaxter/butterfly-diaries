Silver-spotted skipper

Hesperia comma

About the species

The Silver-spotted skipper is a distinctive, fast-flying butterfly associated with warm, open chalk grassland in southern England. It is one of Britain's more specialised butterflies, requiring particular grassland conditions to breed successfully.

Adults are usually on the wing from late July to early September, with numbers often peaking during August. They fly rapidly and close to the ground, frequently settling on bare patches of earth to bask in the sunshine or visiting flowers for nectar. Their preference for warm conditions makes them particularly active on hot, sunny days.

Identification

The Silver-spotted skipper is a small, compact butterfly with a wingspan of approximately 30–36 mm. The upperwings are orange-brown with darker brown borders and pale orange markings. Males have a distinctive dark band running diagonally across the forewings, which helps distinguish them from females.

The undersides are particularly distinctive, with numerous bright silver-white spots set against an olive-green or brownish-green background on the hindwings. These markings give the species its common name and are often the easiest way to confirm an identification.

Like other skippers, it has a characteristic rapid, darting flight. When resting, it often holds its wings in a distinctive half-open posture, making the spotted underside easier to see.

Habitat and distribution

Silver-spotted skippers are strongly associated with open, species-rich chalk grassland, particularly warm, sunny slopes with short, sparse turf and patches of bare ground. They were formerly also found on limestone grassland further north, but their British distribution is now largely restricted to southern England.

Their larval foodplant in Britain is sheep's-fescue (Festuca ovina), a small grass that grows in short, often sparse turf. The caterpillars require individual tufts of this grass, frequently growing close to bare ground where conditions are particularly warm.

Adults feed on a variety of nectar sources, including flowers such as dwarf thistle and other plants found in chalk grassland. They also regularly visit bare ground, where they bask and absorb heat from the sun.

The species has a single generation each year. Females lay eggs individually on the leaves of Sheep's-fescue or occasionally on nearby vegetation during late summer. The eggs overwinter before hatching in spring, with the caterpillars feeding within small silken shelters around the foodplant. Pupation takes place close to the ground, within grass tussocks.

Although the Silver-spotted skipper suffered a substantial historical decline, it has expanded into some previously unoccupied areas in recent decades. However, it remains vulnerable in Britain, with its restricted distribution and dependence on carefully managed chalk grassland making it sensitive to habitat loss and deterioration.
