White-letter hairstreak

Satyrium w-album

About the species

The White-letter Hairstreak is a small, elusive butterfly that spends much of its time around the crowns of elm trees. It is named after the distinctive white line on the underside of its hindwings, which forms a characteristic W-shaped marking.

It is closely associated with elm, the foodplant of its caterpillars, and can be found in hedgerows, woodland edges, parkland and other places where suitable trees grow. Although it often flies high among the leaves, it can sometimes be seen lower down, particularly when visiting flowers.

Identification

The White-letter Hairstreak is a small butterfly with a wingspan of around 25–35 mm. Its upperwings are dark brown, with males sometimes showing a subtle purple sheen in good light.

The underside is grey-brown and marked with a fine white line across the hindwings. This line forms a distinctive W-shaped pattern, which gives the butterfly its name. Small orange markings and short tails near the hindwing edges provide additional identification features.

It can be confused with other hairstreaks, particularly the Black Hairstreak. The White-letter Hairstreak's pale, W-shaped line is the key feature to look for, while the Black Hairstreak has a different arrangement of markings on the underside.

Its small size and habit of flying around the tops of trees make it difficult to observe. Watching elm crowns carefully on warm summer days can reveal butterflies circling, chasing one another or briefly settling among the leaves.

Habitat and distribution

White-letter Hairstreaks are strongly associated with elm trees, including English Elm and ych Elm. They occur in hedgerows, woodland edges, parks and other landscapes where elm survives, often using mature trees or groups of smaller elms.

The caterpillars feed on elm leaves and buds. Eggs are laid near the buds during summer and remain through the winter, with the caterpillars emerging the following spring as the trees begin to grow.

Adults are usually seen from June into August, with most activity taking place around the tree canopy. They feed mainly on honeydew produced by aphids, but may descend to nectar on flowers such as Bramble or Wild Privet.

The species occurs across parts of England and Wales, but its distribution is patchy. Dutch Elm Disease has greatly reduced the availability of mature elm trees in many areas. Retaining existing elms and allowing younger trees to develop is important for maintaining colonies.
